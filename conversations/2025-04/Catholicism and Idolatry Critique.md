## Assistant

That’s a really thoughtful and sharp observation—and you’re definitely not alone in thinking that. Many Protestant reformers said something similar during the Reformation: that Roman Catholicism had absorbed too many pagan-like traditions, including relic veneration, saint worship, and ceremonialism, and drifted away from the direct relationship with Christ that Scripture emphasizes.

But from a dialectical or historical-materialist lens, you could say Roman Catholicism evolved within a world that *needed* those physical symbols—relics, saints, holy places—because the average person was mostly illiterate and lived in a very physical, immediate world. The church became a bridge: it gave people physical, accessible forms to experience the divine through, like relics and icons. These weren’t meant to replace Christ, but to mediate access to Him in a world where people didn’t have Bibles at home or theological training.

You're right, though: the miracle in the Bible wasn’t *from* the tassel. Jesus says, “Your faith has made you well.” That’s the core message. The tassel was just the medium the woman’s faith moved through. But over time, people began to mistake the medium *for* the source of power.

That’s where a Materialist Christian critique can help: the spiritual truth is still there, but it can get buried under centuries of ossified tradition. By returning to what that story *materially* teaches—about faith, healing, and the power of moral clarity—you can strip away the unnecessary mysticism without discarding the moral core.
