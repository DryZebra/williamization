**PART 1: The Material Genesis of the Bible**

### I. What Is the Bible?

Materially, the Bible is a compilation of writings assembled over more than a thousand years, produced in different geographic, linguistic, political, and social environments. It is not a single book but a library of texts, written by humans living in historical conditions, each trying to speak to their own people in ways they would understand.

So what we call “The Bible” is:

- **A collection of cultural memory** preserved by oral tradition before being written.
- **A record of law, myth, genealogy, and prophecy** used to form national and religious identity.
- **A product of political necessity** at various moments—whether to preserve Jewish identity during exile or to consolidate Roman Christianity under Constantine.
- **A technological artifact**—scrolls, then codices, then printings, then translations.

---

### II. The First Words: "In the beginning..."

#### Verse: Genesis 1:1 (KJV)
> *In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.*

This line is not just a statement of cosmology—it’s a declaration of **order and ownership**. And to grasp its weight, we must study three things:

1. **When and why it was written.**
2. **What it meant in its time.**
3. **How it was translated.**

---

### III. WHEN: Babylon and the Birth of Genesis

Genesis wasn’t written *at* the beginning of time—it was written during or after the Babylonian exile (~6th century BCE).

- **The Babylonian Exile (597–539 BCE)**: Judah was conquered, Jerusalem sacked, the Temple destroyed. Jewish elites were taken to Babylon.
- **Crisis**: With the Temple gone, how do you preserve a religion based on Temple worship?
- **Solution**: Write a *book*. Gather oral stories, laws, lineages, and myths into a national memory project.
- **Genesis 1** is a *direct rebuttal* of Babylonian mythology. Where Babylon said chaos gods fought and formed the world out of violence, Genesis says: *No—our God simply spoke.*

#### Material Interpretation:
Genesis 1:1 wasn’t written to tell us how the universe began. It was written to tell exiles: *You still belong to a higher order. You are not Babylonians. Your world has meaning.*

---

### IV. HOW: Translation and Power

Let’s look at **the Hebrew**:

> **Bereshit bara Elohim et hashamayim ve’et ha’aretz.**

- **Bereshit** = “In [the] beginning”
- **Bara** = “[He] created” (used only for divine creation in Hebrew)
- **Elohim** = God (plural form, but takes singular verbs—implying majesty, not polytheism)
- **Et hashamayim ve’et ha’aretz** = “the heavens and the earth”

Note:
- The Hebrew doesn’t say “**the** beginning” definitively. It can mean *“When God began to create…”*
- This opens up the idea that creation was a process, not a one-time boom.

#### Greek (Septuagint ~250 BCE):
> Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἐποίησεν ὁ Θεὸς τὸν οὐρανὸν καὶ τὴν γῆν.

- Now it’s solidly “In *the* beginning God *made* the heavens and the earth.”
- Why? Because the Greek worldview preferred fixed, rational beginnings.
- The Septuagint is the Bible of the early Christians and the early Church.

#### Latin (Vulgate, ~382 CE):
> *In principio creavit Deus caelum et terram.*

- Jerome translated Hebrew directly—but under the authority of the Roman Church.
- “Principio” (principle/origin) strengthens the idea of hierarchy—Rome's favorite idea.

#### English (KJV, 1611 CE):
Commissioned under a **monarch** to solidify **national religion**. Hence:
> *In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.*

- No ambiguity. One moment. One God. One creation. One authority.

#### Material Effect:
Each translation doesn’t just *convey* meaning—it *constructs* it. The text was molded by the political needs of the translator and their time.

---

### V. WHO USED THIS VERSE AND WHY?

- **Ezra (5th century BCE)**: After exile, uses Genesis to rebuild *covenant identity*. Creation implies God’s authority over history.
- **Second Temple scribes**: Creation = *proof of God's justice*. If God made the world, He can judge it.
- **Early Christians**: Use Genesis 1 to connect Jesus to the *Logos*—the Word that creates.
- **Augustine (4th century)**: Declares that Genesis is **allegory**—God exists outside of time. He uses it to bridge pagan Neoplatonism and Christian theology.
- **Martin Luther**: Says Genesis proves the authority of Scripture *over* Church tradition.
- **Modern Fundamentalists (20th century)**: Use Genesis 1:1 to battle evolution—reclaiming the authority of the Bible *over* science.

#### Correct Materialist Understanding:
Genesis 1:1 survives and is repeated because it *asserts that the world is knowable, ordered, and good*. That’s useful in *every era* of class struggle—when people feel powerless, oppressed, or uprooted. The verse *materially helps people reorient themselves toward meaning and purpose*.
