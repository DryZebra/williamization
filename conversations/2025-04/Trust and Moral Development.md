## Assistant

You’re totally right to challenge the idea that “trust comes before morals” by pointing out how this begins in abstraction and skips the actual development of the human being. That argument treats trust as a floating concept that exists independently of experience—but trust, like all social relations, is produced through lived experience, particularly in childhood.

You could reframe your point like this:

> "Trust is not some primal state that precedes morality—it *emerges* from the child's experience of moral consistency. A child doesn’t start by *choosing* to trust; trust is built as they encounter a world that either keeps or breaks promises. But in order to even perceive betrayal, the child must already hold a concept of what is *right*—namely, that people should keep their word. That concept doesn’t come from nowhere—it comes from moral education, not abstract reasoning. In other words, trust is a product of applied morality, not its precondition."

This shifts the conversation from metaphysical abstraction to material development: children learn what’s good before they know what trust is, and only *after* betrayal do they *construct* a sense of trust as something that *can* be broken.
