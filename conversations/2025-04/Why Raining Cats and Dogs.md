What's the origin of he phrase "it's raining cats and dogs?"

---

**Surface Etymology**  
The phrase appears as early as the 1650s–1700s in England. There's no definitive origin, but there are three main theories:  
- **Literal debris theory:** During heavy storms in old cities, trash, dead animals like cats and dogs, etc., would get washed through the streets. So "raining cats and dogs" might come from storms causing chaos, filth, and flooding — making it seem like cats and dogs were being poured from the sky.  
- **Folklore theory:** Some Norse myths link cats to rain (cats influencing storms) and dogs to wind (dogs representing Odin’s hunting hounds). So “cats and dogs” could just mean “storm.”  
- **Misheard/metaphor theory:** It could be a bastardization of older phrases like “cats and dogs” being slang for “violence,” or mishearing Greek phrases meaning "an unlikely event."  

But honestly, none of that fully explains why *this* phrase stuck.

---

**Why It Stuck — Deeper Reason**  
1. **Vivid Image**:  
Language *loves* striking, absurd images. “Raining hard” sounds boring. "Raining cats and dogs" creates a vivid, cartoonish, absurd mental picture. You **see** it immediately. It’s memorable.

2. **Absurdity Mirrors Reality**:  
English speakers (especially in England) love absurd, exaggerated understatement and humor. The weather being miserable is *normal*, but framing it ridiculously (“cats and dogs falling from the sky”) is a way of emotionally handling how common, awful weather feels.

3. **Animal Familiarity**:  
Cats and dogs are the two most common domestic animals. Every peasant, merchant, noble — everyone in England knew cats and dogs. If you said “it’s raining pigs and geese,” it wouldn’t land the same way. Cats and dogs fight, cause chaos, fill households. The phrase piggybacks off their ubiquity and conflict.

4. **Sonic Stickiness**:  
“Cats and dogs” rolls off the tongue. Hard consonants (C, D) and short vowels make it punchy. It's a perfect little rhythm, almost like a nursery rhyme.

5. **Urbanization and Street Imagery**:  
As English cities got denser and filthier during the early modern period (1500s–1700s), urban life was chaotic, gross, unpredictable. Floods, dead animals, chamber pots — bad weather **felt** like living chaos. "Raining cats and dogs" metaphorically captured that messy, smelly, overwhelming experience.

---

**In short:**  
> It stuck because it's **vivid**, **absurd yet relatable**, **built on common knowledge**, and **perfectly rhythmic**.  
> It mirrors real experiences of chaos and disgust in a way that’s easier to laugh about.
