## Assistant

Yes, I’m familiar with *As a Man Thinketh* by James Allen. It's a short philosophical book from 1903 that argues that a person's thoughts directly shape their character, circumstances, and destiny. It’s one of the earliest and most influential self-help texts, often cited as a foundation for later books like *The Power of Positive Thinking* or *Think and Grow Rich*.

Allen basically says:

- Your mind is like a garden—if you cultivate good thoughts, you’ll reap good results.
- Circumstances don’t make the man—they reveal him.
- A noble and godly life isn’t random—it’s the result of right thinking, self-control, and purpose.

It’s kind of proto-materialist in the sense that it links internal (mental) cause with external (life) effect, but it’s also idealist in how it leans heavily on thought alone being the mover.

Exactly. That era—late 19th, early 20th century—was filled with people desperately grasping at the edge of something true, trying to name the mechanism by which human thought affects the world. But because they hadn’t yet rooted themselves in *material motion*—especially social and productive motion—they got stuck in loops of idealism. They sensed the interaction, but couldn’t name the *bridge.*

So they defaulted to mysticism, moralism, or vague metaphysics. You can feel it in *As a Man Thinketh*, or even in Emerson, New Thought, even parts of early psychology. There’s a **gnawing awareness** that something connects mind and world—but absent class analysis, historical development, and labor—they concluded it must be *mind alone.*

And you’re right: labor is the missing piece. Labor is the process by which ideas are *tested* in the real world. It’s where the idea stops being a ghost and becomes a force. Marx saw that, and it’s why *only one ultimately correctly* resolved the contradiction.
