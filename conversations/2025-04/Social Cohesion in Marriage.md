## Assistant

You're on exactly the right track by beginning with *social cohesion* as the material foundation for marriage, not simply moral proclamation. The Bible reflects this evolving dialectic—marriage begins as an institution of *property, alliance, and survival*, and is gradually re-formed by historical conditions into an institution of *unity, love, and social responsibility*.

Let’s trace that dialectically:

---

### 1. **The Early Phase: Patriarchal Property and Alliance**
- **Leviticus 20, Exodus 21, Deuteronomy 21** reflect a society where:
  - Women are *literal property*, transferred by bride price or inheritance.
  - Marriage is arranged by fathers, ensuring lineage and tribal survival.
  - Polygyny is permissible not because it’s good, but because it *suits the material needs* of agrarian patriarchy (e.g. managing labor, inheritance, and male dominance).
  - The *purpose* of marriage was survival and reproduction under conditions of tribal warfare and scarcity.

> **Materialist point:** This was not a divine endorsement, but the reflection of class and gender dynamics necessary for Israelite survival and expansion. Women were not equal subjects in the covenant—only men were—and thus polygyny wasn't about love or virtue, but social engineering under conditions of inequality.

---

### 2. **Dialectical Progress: Prophetic Challenges to Exploitation**
- The *prophets* begin shifting this. Hosea and Malachi both emphasize *faithfulness and justice* in marriage.
- Malachi 2:14–16: “The Lord has been a witness between you and the wife of your youth… Do not be unfaithful to her.”

> **Materialist reading:** Faithfulness begins to displace polygyny as *the new moral requirement* due to changing material conditions. As Israel consolidates into a post-exilic nation with limited territory, family units shrink, and emotional bonds become central.

---

### 3. **New Testament Shift: Spiritual Equality and Mutual Love**
- Jesus *never* promotes polygyny. He points back to **Genesis 2:24**, emphasizing *two becoming one flesh*—a monogamous model rooted in unity and sacrificial love.
- **Matthew 19:8–9**: "Moses permitted you to divorce because your hearts were hard. But it was not this way from the beginning."
- Paul in **Ephesians 5** introduces mutual *submission* and *Christlike love* as the new foundation of marriage.
- **1 Timothy 3:2** and **Titus 1:6** declare that bishops must be the “husband of one wife.” Why? Because the early Church’s *moral authority* depends on showing personal restraint and family stability.

> **Materialist reading:** As Christianity emerges in Roman society, the material conditions change again. Marriage becomes the **cell of community morality** and Church leadership is predicated on emotional maturity, restraint, and unity—not tribal accumulation of wives or heirs.

---

### 4. **Materialist Christian View of Marriage**
> Marriage is not sacred because of any one fixed definition. It is sacred because it binds people together for mutual good, social cohesion, and generational stability. It is a **dynamic institution** shaped by material need and re-evaluated through moral dialectic.

#### So what do we say about polygyny?
- It was a **historically necessary** structure under certain conditions.
- But in Christianity, **the moral dialectic evolved** toward *unity, equality, and love* as material conditions changed.
- Therefore, while the Bible contains polygyny, it does not **endorse** it for Christians today—it **transcends** it, through evolving practice and higher moral synthesis.

---

If someone insists on polygyny as a “Biblical model,” ask:
> Why did Jesus and Paul both center marriage on *one man and one woman*?
> Why does the New Testament limit Church leaders to monogamy if polygyny were still moral?
> Do they believe Christianity should reflect *early tribal customs* or the *full arc of moral development* that emerged through Christ?

---

### Final Word:
> Materialist Christianity doesn’t worship *form*, it reveres *motion*. Marriage is sacred not because of how it looked in 1200 BCE, but because of what it becomes when shaped by love, responsibility, and dialectical growth.
