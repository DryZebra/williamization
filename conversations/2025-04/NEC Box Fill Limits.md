# NEC Box Fill Limits

## Assistant

You're right to push back on that a little — your apprentice is technically correct that the **NEC does not explicitly limit the number of extension rings** you can stack on a box. However, **there are practical and code-based constraints** that still apply. Here’s the breakdown:

### 1. **Box Fill Calculations Still Apply**
No matter how many rings you stack, **you still have to calculate total volume and box fill per NEC 314.16(B)**. If the total volume (box + extensions) isn't enough for the conductors, clamps, devices, etc., it's a violation. So while stacking rings adds volume, it doesn’t mean you can ignore the fill limits.

### 2. **Mechanical Integrity and Support**
Per **NEC 314.40**, boxes and extension rings must be **securely fastened**. Excessively stacked rings can compromise the mechanical integrity of the installation, especially in environments subject to vibration or movement.

Some AHJs or job specs will cite **Article 110.3(B)** (listing and labeling instructions) to restrict the number of rings if it exceeds what the assembly is listed for. If the manufacturer doesn’t allow more than one or two rings, stacking more could violate the listing.

### 3. **Workmanship and Accessibility**
If you stack too many rings, **the depth may make it impractical to access devices or perform maintenance**, which could violate the **general workmanship clause in 110.12**. If the box is behind drywall, for example, code requires that it be flush (314.20), and stacking could cause it to protrude or recede improperly.

---

### In Practice:
- **One or two rings** is common and generally acceptable.
- **Three or more rings** starts to raise flags — it may pass in some places, but inspectors might fail it based on 110.12 or manufacturer's listing limits.

So while there's no hard **numerical limit** in the NEC, you're on solid ground saying **you can’t just keep stacking them forever**.
