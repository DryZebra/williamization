---

### **THE PASSAGE: Luke 7:36–50 (Summary)**  
A “sinful woman” washes Jesus' feet with her tears, dries them with her hair, and anoints them with perfume. Simon, a Pharisee, questions Jesus allowing such a woman to touch him. Jesus tells a parable: two debtors owed different amounts, and both were forgiven—who would love more? Simon answers, “The one who was forgiven more.” Jesus confirms this and explains the woman’s great love as a reflection of great forgiveness.

---

### **DIALECTICAL BREAKDOWN**

#### **Thesis (Mosaic Law / Old Covenant morality)**  
Under the Old Covenant (especially Leviticus, Deuteronomy), sin was measured, categorized, and punished in proportion. Social and ritual purity were paramount. Forgiveness came at a price—sacrifice, exclusion, and often permanent social consequence.  
**Sin = impurity = separation.**  
The moral logic was transactional, juridical, and external.

---

#### **Antithesis (The Personal Guilt of the Outcast)**  
As society developed, stories like those of **David and Bathsheba**, **Hosea and the prostitute**, and **Job’s suffering** introduced contradictions. Was guilt purely legal? Or was love, contrition, and trust in God more important?

The Psalms (especially Psalm 51) reflect this inner shift:  
> “You do not delight in sacrifice, or I would bring it… a broken and contrite heart you, God, will not despise.”  
A seed is planted: **perhaps forgiveness isn't transactional.**

Yet, the people most hurt—prostitutes, tax collectors, lepers—are still left out of moral society.

---

#### **Synthesis (Jesus’ Action in Luke 7)**  
Jesus embodies a **materialist moral revolution**. He takes the contradiction—**legal guilt vs. internal love**—and resolves it in favor of love as the measure of forgiveness.

The woman isn’t forgiven *despite* her sin, nor is she forgiven *after* a sacrifice.  
She is forgiven because her **act of love** reveals the **truth** of her transformation.

> “Her many sins have been forgiven—as her great love has shown.”

This is a total reversal of the prior logic: **Love isn’t the reward of forgiveness. It’s the evidence of it.**

---

### **MATERIALIST CHRISTIAN READING**  
From a dialectical materialist lens, this passage shows **morality evolving from legal objectivity to social subjectivity**. Jesus isn’t abolishing the Law, but **transcending** it by rooting morality in observable social action—*not ritual, but relation.*

He isn’t saying “forget the Law,” but “the Law was pointing to this.”  
This passage is a **synthesis** of:
- **the law-based external morality of Moses**, and  
- **the internalized, compassionate cry of the prophets and psalmists.**

The outcome is a morality that is:
- **material** (shown in real acts of love),  
- **dialectical** (arising from conflict between law and lived experience),  
- and **universal** (accessible even to the most condemned).

  > “I was thinking about this passage—how do you see it building on earlier parts of Scripture?”

- **Let Them Teach**  
  Give space for others to bring what they know. Let them process aloud.  
  > “What do you think Jesus is showing here?”  
  > “Have you ever connected this with another part of the Bible?”

- **Encourage Movement, Not Conclusions**  
  Your goal isn’t to land an argument—it’s to invite reflection that continues even after the conversation ends. The more someone explains their position, the more they begin to discover its deeper origins.

## **The River Lens**  
*A Way of Viewing Scripture and Belief*

The River Lens is about seeing ideas not as fixed points, but as things that *develop over time*. It’s not concerned with proving or disproving God. It focuses on *how truths formed,* and *what shaped them.*

- **Ideas Flow from Real Conditions**  
  Justice, forgiveness, sacrifice, love—these didn’t appear fully formed. They were shaped by changing lives, changing needs, and changing understandings.  
  > “In the Old Testament, forgiveness often came through sacrifice. By Luke 7, it comes through love. What do you think led to that shift?”

- **Don’t Debate Origins—Observe Movement**  
  You’re not asking whether the river is divine or man-made. You’re just reading its motion. Let the story tell its own journey.

- **Honor the Shaping Forces**  
  The river flows differently when it hits rock, sand, or a man-made dam. The same is true of moral development. Pressure creates turns. Resistance shapes banks. Pay attention to where those moments happen in Scripture.

## **Why This Works**

- **Christians feel respected**—because you’re not questioning their faith, you’re asking for their insight. You’re taking Scripture seriously and encouraging deeper reading.  
- **Atheists feel engaged**—because you’re grounding belief in real, observable development, not asking for supernatural leaps.  
- **Truth becomes inevitable**—because when people walk the path and look through the lens, they start to recognize the shape of truth unfolding on its own.

## **Closing Thought: Walking Beside the River**  
I don’t tell the river where to go. I walk beside it.  
I notice when it flows smoothly and when it churns. When it turns back on itself or rises uphill, I don’t assume it’s wrong—I ask what shaped it. What pressures built up? What events redirected it? What truths carved its banks?

Materialist Christianity doesn’t prescribe a flow. It traces the movement. It listens to the current. It walks the path that changes as the river changes.

Because truth isn’t something we impose. It’s something we uncover—layer by layer, turn by turn—by walking together and asking better questions.
