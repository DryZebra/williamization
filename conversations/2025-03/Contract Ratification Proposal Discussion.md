## Assistant

From the outside looking in, the statement appears to be an attempt at striking a balance between appeasing those calling for member ratification and preserving the leadership’s long-established control. Here’s how and why it may be achieving that:

1. **Restricting Ratification to “Solely Local 553” Agreements**  
   - By limiting ratification strictly to those agreements “negotiated solely by Local 553,” the leadership is essentially exempting a significant portion of the contracts—particularly large, multi-union agreements—from any membership vote.  
   - This means that some of the most consequential or far-reaching contracts (like Outside Construction or Telecom) would proceed as normal, negotiated and settled primarily by the leadership without an actual membership veto.

2. **Specially Called Ratification Meetings**  
   - Mandating a “specially called ratification meeting” can control the timing and venue for ratification votes.  
   - When leadership controls the logistics—like how soon the meeting is called, how it’s advertised, and what procedures govern it—they shape who actually shows up and how the debate is conducted. Fewer people often attend special-called meetings than general or regularly scheduled ones, which can benefit leadership-friendly participants.

3. **“Preserving the Option to Request the Right to Settle”**  
   - The language suggests that even if a ratification clause were adopted, the local’s leaders could still “request the right to settle in future negotiations.”  
   - This carve-out keeps leadership’s ultimate authority intact. Should they deem full member ratification too cumbersome or risky in certain negotiations, they can invoke this provision and essentially bypass a vote.

4. **Positioning Itself as Being Open-Minded**  
   - The message is framed as “not opposed to ratification” but full of “caution” and “balance.” In reality, it threads the needle between appearing receptive to democracy in bargaining and preserving existing hierarchy and authority structures.  
   - Leaders can claim they have taken membership’s concern for greater involvement “seriously,” but the restrictions and conditions on that involvement ensure the final say often remains with the leadership.

5. **Minimizing Disruption to Existing Power Dynamics**  
   - By citing the local’s “elected leadership structure”—with representatives chosen to bargain every three years—he reinforces the idea that members already “have their say” by electing a negotiating team, and further ratification might be only for show (or for smaller-scale contracts).  
   - This framing keeps the existing chain of command intact and presents broader membership ratification as an “add-on” that can be toggled off for the most important or multi-union contracts.

---

**In essence**: He is trying to meet the request for ratification partway—enough to say there is member involvement—while structuring the process to ensure leadership retains practical control over the most critical negotiations. Rather than a wide-open, binding membership vote on all contracts, it’s a controlled process that leaves leadership an “opt-out” on bigger deals and allows them to choreograph the conditions under which a vote takes place.

## Assistant

Below is a sample response that outwardly appears to address his concerns (i.e., that certain agreements remain under leadership discretion), yet subtly expands the scope of “jurisdictional agreements” so that nearly everything goes to the membership for a vote. In effect, you’re playing up the need for “clarification” but using that clarification to push ratification far beyond what he’s suggesting. 

---

**Subject: Re: Proposed Bylaw Language on Ratification**

Brother [Name],

Thank you for including the Executive Board and for taking the time to share your perspective on how ratification might function. I fully agree that any bylaw language must reflect Local 553’s structure and reality—especially respecting the elected leadership while ensuring members have their voices heard.

In that spirit, I want to build upon your idea of “jurisdictional agreements” being ratified through a specially called meeting. To clarify and ensure it is workable in practice, I suggest we define *jurisdictional agreements* in the broadest terms that cover all scenarios where Local 553 members’ wages, benefits, or working conditions are affected. That way, we can capture precisely those contracts for which our Local has standing, whether or not we negotiate them “solo” or in tandem with other unions. 

Here is potential clarifying language (for discussion):

> “All jurisdictional agreements—defined as any collective bargaining agreement, memorandum of understanding, or other binding contract directly impacting wages, benefits, or working conditions of Local 553 members—shall be brought before the membership for ratification at a specially called ratification meeting. A majority of members present and voting shall decide.”

This approach accomplishes several things:

1. **Respects the Leadership Structure**  
   Our elected negotiators still conduct day-to-day bargaining and frame tentative agreements, but now they enjoy the added strength of knowing the membership will stand behind (or reject) the final product. 

2. **Ensures Member Involvement**  
   A specially called meeting for ratification means only those who choose to participate vote on whether to adopt the agreement. This encourages active engagement while respecting that leadership must manage day-to-day talks.

3. **Preserves Flexibility in Multi-Union Agreements**  
   Even where multiple unions are at the table, we would ratify *our* Local’s portion, *if* it has a direct impact on wages, benefits, or working conditions of our members. This preserves the solidarity of multi-union efforts but also ensures our members’ interests are never signed away without their voice.

4. **Allows for Opt-Out or Streamlining When Needed**  
   If at some future point leadership and members collectively see a need to expedite negotiations, we could propose language allowing the membership to pre-authorize a settlement. This would be done on a case-by-case basis rather than in the bylaws themselves, respecting the membership’s control.

I believe these clarifications offer a balanced and transparent process, ensuring leadership retains its negotiating role while guaranteeing that the final say rests with the members most affected by any agreement. I welcome your feedback and look forward to continued discussion so we can craft language that works best for our Local.

In Solidarity,

[Your Name]
Local 553 Member

### Why This Works

1. **You Appear to “Agree”**  
   By beginning with warm acknowledgment of his concerns and the importance of leadership, you soften resistance. You aren’t blatantly rejecting his points.

2. **You Redefine “Jurisdictional Agreements”**  
   His language limits ratification to “agreements negotiated solely by Local 553.” In your version, *any agreement affecting Local 553 members’ wages or conditions* automatically qualifies—thus pulling virtually all significant contracts into membership ratification.

3. **You Keep the “Specially Called Meeting”**  
   On paper, you’re accepting his structure for calling a vote, which seems to cater to his preference. However, by applying that structure to *every* agreement that matters, you’re turning it into a comprehensive ratification process.

4. **You Use the “Right to Settle” as a Future Option**  
   Rather than outright rejecting the idea of letting leadership finalize deals, you simply say that can still happen—if the membership explicitly votes for it case by case. This makes it unlikely leadership can claim you ignored their concern for flexibility.

In short, you’re giving him the official structure he wants but broadening it so that nearly everything goes up for a membership vote. On its face, it appears cooperative. Functionally, it’s “full” ratification.
