#### **The Passage (KJV)**
> **15** And in those days Peter stood up in the midst of the disciples, and said, (the number of names together were about an hundred and twenty,)  
> **16** Men and brethren, this scripture must needs have been fulfilled, which the Holy Ghost by the mouth of David spake before concerning Judas, which was guide to them that took Jesus.

---

## **1. Literary and Theological Context**
This passage is a crucial moment in **Acts**, which serves as a bridge between the Gospel accounts and the early formation of the Christian Church. The Book of Acts is traditionally attributed to **Luke**, who also wrote the Gospel of Luke. His writing is concerned with **continuity**—how the work of Jesus extends into the early Christian movement. 

### **Key Themes in Acts 1:15-20**
1. **Leadership Transition** – The disciples are dealing with the betrayal and death of Judas, recognizing the need to replace him for the leadership of the early Christian movement.
2. **Fulfillment of Scripture** – Peter argues that Judas’ fate was prophesied, citing **Psalm 69:25** and **Psalm 109:8**.
3. **Judas’ Death and Consequences** – This passage portrays Judas' betrayal as having resulted in his own destruction and the symbolic naming of Aceldama, the "Field of Blood."
4. **Authority of Peter** – This marks Peter stepping into a clear leadership role, guiding the disciples through this transitional period.

---

## **2. Relation to Other Biblical Books**
### **Old Testament Parallels**
- **Psalm 69:25** ("Let his habitation be desolate…") is traditionally read as a lament of a righteous sufferer who is surrounded by enemies. Peter interprets it as foreshadowing Judas' fate.
- **Psalm 109:8** ("Let another take his office") provides justification for appointing a new apostle.

### **Gospels’ Perspective on Judas**
- **Matthew 27:3-10** presents a different account of Judas' death—he hangs himself, and the priests buy the "Field of Blood" rather than Judas purchasing it.
- **John 17:12** refers to Judas as "the son of perdition," emphasizing his role in fulfilling divine prophecy.

### **Acts’ Forward Narrative**
This event sets the stage for Pentecost (Acts 2), where the Holy Spirit descends upon the disciples, marking the true beginning of the Christian Church.

---

## **3. Historical Materialist Context**
From a **historical materialist** perspective, this passage can be understood in terms of:
1. **The Class Position of the Disciples** – The early Christian movement largely consisted of poor, marginalized, or working-class individuals (fishermen, tax collectors, tradespeople). Judas' betrayal, often framed as an act of greed, could instead be seen through the material conditions—did he betray Jesus out of sheer malice, or were there deeper economic and political pressures?
2. **Roman Occupation and Jewish Resistance** – The disciples were part of a group that saw Jesus as a revolutionary figure in a colonized Judea under Roman rule. The movement was essentially anti-imperial and subversive.
3. **Judas as a ‘Class Traitor’** – In a Marxist framework, Judas’ role could be compared to a **traitor to the working-class struggle**—a figure who sides with the ruling class (Roman and Jewish elite) to maintain the status quo.
4. **The Need for Organizational Continuity** – Peter’s insistence on replacing Judas reflects a material necessity in any movement: leadership vacancies must be filled for the movement to continue effectively.

Thus, a **materialist interpretation** sees this passage not as mere fulfillment of divine prophecy but as an example of how movements adapt after internal crises, how leadership shifts, and how ideological narratives (such as prophecy) serve to justify necessary organizational changes.

---

## **4. Conclusion: The Message and Materialist Takeaway**
The passage serves **two primary functions**:
1. **Theological Justification** – It frames Judas' betrayal and death as predestined, reinforcing divine control over history.
2. **Political/Organizational Stabilization** – The apostles needed to maintain leadership and legitimacy, so Peter reinterprets scripture to justify moving forward with a replacement.

### **Materialist Conclusion**
A materialist reading suggests that the transition of leadership in Acts 1:15-20 reflects a **common problem in revolutionary movements**—when a key figure betrays or abandons the cause, the group must recover and reorganize to maintain momentum. Peter's speech is, at its core, **an attempt to maintain ideological unity and prevent factionalism** in the wake of Judas’ betrayal. 

This aligns with the broader historical reality of early Christianity: it was a **persecuted, grassroots movement** that needed strong internal cohesion to survive under both Jewish and Roman opposition. The passage thus reflects the dialectical necessity of **restructuring leadership after internal betrayal**, a common phenomenon in revolutionary and political movements throughout history.

Numbers 11 serves **two main functions**:
1. **Theological Justification** – It explains why the Israelites faced hardships and emphasizes the importance of obedience.
2. **Political/Organizational Necessity** – It legitimizes the **shift from single-person leadership (Moses) to a more distributed structure**, ensuring the survival of the Israelite society.

### **Materialist Conclusion**
A historical materialist reading suggests that **Numbers 11 reflects a society in transition**, dealing with:
- **Scarcity-driven unrest**
- **The evolution of leadership structures**
- **The role of ideological control (divine punishment) in maintaining order**
