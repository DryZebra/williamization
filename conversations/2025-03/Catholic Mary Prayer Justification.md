That's a really good question, and you're definitely noticing a key difference: Catholic theology developed under *different material conditions* than Protestant theology, and that shaped how it sees Mary, the saints, and the role of mediation in general.

Let’s break it down in both **Biblical references** and **material/historical context**, so you can engage meaningfully from a Materialist Christian perspective.

---

### **1. The Biblical Justification for Praying to Mary and the Saints**
Catholics don’t see themselves as praying *instead of* to God—they believe they are asking the saints to *intercede* for them **to** God. It’s more like asking a righteous person to pray for you, even after their death.

Here are the key verses they often cite:

#### **Luke 1:28 (Hail Mary)**
> “Hail, full of grace, the Lord is with thee.”
- Used as part of the **Hail Mary** prayer.
- Catholics see this as evidence that Mary is uniquely graced by God and thus worthy of veneration.

#### **Luke 1:43**
> “And why is this granted to me, that the mother of my Lord should come to me?”
- Elizabeth calling Mary “mother of my Lord” is interpreted to elevate her role.

#### **John 2:1–11 (Wedding at Cana)**
> Jesus performs His first miracle at Mary’s request.
- Catholics interpret this as evidence that Mary can intercede effectively with Jesus.

#### **Revelation 5:8**
> “The twenty-four elders fell down before the Lamb, each holding a harp, and golden bowls full of incense, *which are the prayers of the saints*.”
- Used to justify the idea that saints are *actively participating* in interceding with God.

#### **Revelation 8:3–4**
> “Another angel came… and was given much incense to offer with the prayers of all the saints.”
- Again, seen as showing heavenly beings involved in bringing prayers before God.

#### **James 5:16**
> “The prayer of a righteous person has great power as it is working.”
- Catholics argue saints are righteous and alive in Christ, so they can still pray effectively for others.

---

### **2. Material and Historical Development**
From a **Materialist Christian** view, the veneration of saints and Mary grew out of *real social and spiritual needs*:

- **Under persecution**, early Christians found strength in remembering the martyrs. The cult of the saints helped create solidarity and identity.
- **Illiteracy** was widespread. Saints were “living examples” of the Gospel, accessible to ordinary people through story, image, and prayer.
- **God was distant** in many theological frameworks. The saints and Mary offered more *personal*, *compassionate* intermediaries—especially in a feudal world where mediation was how power flowed.
- Mary in particular was elevated as a motherly figure of divine compassion at a time when God the Father was depicted as increasingly abstract, kingly, or wrathful.
- **Pilgrimage culture** and **local patronage** further strengthened this, creating a material *network* of saint-veneration that sustained communities and economies.

In short: **Catholic practices evolved to meet the spiritual, emotional, and social needs of people in a feudal, hierarchical society**—not because they misread the Bible, but because they *materially interpreted the Gospel through their world*.
