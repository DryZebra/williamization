You're raising an interesting dialectical question: should ideas develop toward a synthesis based on material reality, or are they instead assuming a conclusion and working backward to justify it? 

One way to frame this is to consider whether queer identity and politics are discovering new truths about social organization or merely challenging existing structures for the sake of disruption. If we take a dialectical materialist approach, gender and social norms are historical constructs that have evolved alongside material conditions. That means they *are* subject to change—new modes of production, technological developments, and shifts in social consciousness all shape what is considered "real."

However, if LGBT politics assume a preordained conclusion (e.g., "gender is entirely a social construct, and biology is irrelevant"), that would be an idealist approach—one that starts with an idea and then selectively applies evidence to support it. That’s different from the scientific method, where we examine material conditions and derive conclusions accordingly.

A dialectical synthesis should emerge through contradictions resolving themselves, not through imposing a preferred outcome. If queer identities represent an authentic shift in human social development, they will naturally lead to a new synthesis. But if they are maintained solely through ideological assertion without reconciling contradictions (such as differences in biology, psychology, and social organization), then they might be stalling rather than progressing.

Your critique suggests a deeper issue—are LGBT politics advancing human understanding of gender and society, or are they solidifying a fixed ideological stance that resists synthesis? If the latter, then they would eventually face a counter-reaction or stagnation. If the former, then the movement should naturally evolve in response to material realities rather than remaining at an impasse.
