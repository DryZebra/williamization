Absolutely. Marx’s concept of **species-being (Gattungswesen)** is one of the most powerful bridges between Marxism and Christianity—especially a *Materialist Christianity* that sees Christianity as a discovery rather than a declaration.

### What is Species-Being?

For Marx, **species-being** refers to what makes humans distinct from animals. It’s not intelligence or tool use—it’s that humans can consciously shape their world. We don't just live in nature; we *produce* our world, and through this, we express ourselves. Our essence is not just to survive, but to **create, relate, labor, and care**, together. He writes in the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*:

> “Man is a species-being, not only because he practically and theoretically makes the species his object… but also because he treats himself as the actual, living species.”

This means:
- We’re **social beings**, not meant to be atomized.
- We’re **creative beings**, meant to labor meaningfully.
- We’re **moral beings**, driven toward justice, unity, and care—not because of laws, but because that’s who we are when we aren’t alienated.

### How This Contributes to Materialist Christianity

Now connect that to Christianity:
- Christianity says humans are made “in the image of God.” Marx would say: we are made to reflect our **species-being**—to love, build, care, and co-create.
- Sin (in a materialist frame) becomes **alienation**—from each other, from our labor, from our essence.
- Salvation is **the overcoming of alienation**—through love, solidarity, humility, forgiveness, and the formation of a just community.
- Christ, then, becomes the **perfect image of unalienated man**—a man who lives fully in his species-being: healing, forgiving, serving, and calling others into communion.

In this view, Christianity becomes the **spiritual intuition of species-being**, spoken before we had the material language to describe it.

### In Short:
- **Species-being** gives you the *what* of human morality: social, cooperative, creative, just.
- **Materialist Christianity** says: Christianity intuited that truth long before Marx named it.
- Your morals are not commanded from above—they’re revealed from within.

### Was Marx a Lutheran?

Yes, Marx was born into a Lutheran family in Trier, Germany. His father converted from Judaism to Protestantism, and Marx grew up in a culture shaped by **German idealism**, **Lutheran moral rigor**, and the **aftermath of the Reformation**. He knew the Bible well. In fact, his early writings are full of **religious imagery and themes**—alienation, redemption, the fall of man, etc.

But Marx didn’t see Christianity as a truth to build on—instead, he saw it as a *distorted reflection* of material truths. In *Critique of Hegel’s Philosophy of Right*, he wrote:

> “Religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world… It is the opium of the people.”

This doesn’t mean religion is just a lie—it means religion reflects **real suffering** and **real longings**, but expresses them **through myth and illusion**, not science or clear material analysis.

---

### Why Didn't Marx See Christianity as a Discovery of Species-Being?

1. **He was fighting real religious power**: In 19th century Europe, Christianity was an ally of the ruling class—churches upheld monarchy, private property, patriarchy. So Marx had to **break with the religious institutions** of his day to reach the people’s truth beneath them.

2. **He lived before dialectical theology**: Thinkers like Karl Barth, Dietrich Bonhoeffer, and liberation theologians hadn’t yet reframed Christianity as something revolutionary and grounded in history. So Marx saw religion more like Hegel did: as a philosophical abstraction, not a developing historical process.

3. **He didn’t live long enough to witness Christianity’s material turn**: He died in 1883—long before the rise of liberation theology, the Christian Socialists, or theologians influenced by Marx himself. **You**, in contrast, are standing at the end of that historical development, and you can look back and say: “Wait—was Christianity actually *revealing* our species-being this whole time?”

### 1. Christ, the Unalienated Human

Materially speaking, what do we see in Christ’s life?

- **He worked** (as a carpenter).
- **He taught and healed** (freely, without price).

In all of this, we’re not just seeing a religious figure—we’re seeing what Marx might have called the **realization of species-being**: a human acting freely, in loving relation with others, driven not by profit or status, but by purpose, compassion, and truth.

### 2. Marx’s Species-Being: Humanity at Its Best

For Marx, alienation comes when humans are cut off from their **true nature**—from meaningful work, from other people, from the products of their labor, and even from themselves. This is what capitalism does: it severs us from our **essence**.

Species-being, then, is not some abstract idea—it is **our potential**: to live in conscious, cooperative creation with others. It is found in:

- **Mutual care**  
- **Creative labor**  
- **Communal living**  
- **Recognition of others as ends, not means**  

Now look back at Christ. Isn’t that the life He modeled?

### 3. The Historical Jesus and Material Morality

Historically, Jesus was a Jewish preacher living under **Roman imperial occupation**. He spoke to the **working class, the sick, the tax-burdened, the colonized**. He was:

- Preaching a **Kingdom of God** not based on Caesar’s sword.
- Telling rich men to **give away their wealth**.
- Dining with the **undesirable and forgotten**.
- Teaching that **the first shall be last** and **the meek shall inherit the earth**.

His life and death were not only theological events—they were deeply political and material. He was **killed by the state** for challenging its legitimacy.

> In this light, the crucifixion is not just sacrifice—it’s the logical result of a human being **refusing alienation** in a world built on exploitation.

### 4. Christianity as the Intuition of Species-Essence

Before we had dialectical materialism, before sociology or psychology, we had parables. We had beatitudes. We had the moral grammar of a world struggling toward liberation. Christianity became the vessel for our **intuitive understanding** of what it means to live rightly, communally, and fully human.

- “Love your neighbor as yourself” — mutual recognition.  
- “Whatever you do to the least of these, you do to me” — unity of personhood.  
- “Blessed are the poor” — moral reversal of class power.

These are not *commands from the sky*—they are *discovered truths*, born in the material pain and hope of an oppressed people. They resonate because they are **what we long for**, even now.

Marx saw religion as a kind of dream: beautiful, but mistaken. But what if Christianity wasn’t a dream—but an **early, poetic glimpse** of the same truth Marx was uncovering? What if Christ wasn’t a myth hiding truth, but a *person who revealed it*—not metaphysically, but materially?

- The **Church** becomes a flawed, historic attempt at **communion in labor and love**.
- And Christ becomes the **first clear expression** of humanity’s species-essence.

### 6. Conclusion: Why This Matters

If Christianity intuited what Marx explained, then we don’t have to throw away the Cross to find justice—we just have to look at it **through new eyes**. The Incarnation tells us that **truth is not outside this world**, but within it—inside human nature, revealed through our labor, our love, our struggles, and our solidarity.

Materialist Christianity doesn’t baptize capitalism or strip away faith—it **restores Christ** to His rightful place: the human who lived fully, who revealed what it means to be free, and who calls us to do the same.
