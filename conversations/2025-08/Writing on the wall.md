The story you’re thinking of is from **Daniel 5** in the Old Testament. It’s the famous tale of *“the writing on the wall”* during King Belshazzar’s feast in Babylon. As the king and his nobles drank from the vessels taken from the Temple in Jerusalem, suddenly “the fingers of a man’s hand” appeared and wrote on the plaster of the palace wall. The words were mysterious—“MENE, MENE, TEKEL, PARSIN”—and Daniel was summoned to interpret them. He explained that God had numbered Belshazzar’s days, weighed him and found him wanting, and divided his kingdom, which was about to fall to the Medes and Persians.

---

### Scientific Explanations (Naturalistic Readings)
Scholars and skeptics over the centuries have proposed different “scientific” or material explanations for the event, such as:

1. **Optical Illusion / Light Projection**  
   Flickering oil lamps, reflective surfaces, or smoke could have made shadows look like writing on the wall. In a state of intoxication, this could have seemed supernatural.

2. **Hallucination**  
   Belshazzar and his nobles were drinking heavily. Alcohol, possible contaminated wine, or ritual additives (like psychoactive herbs) might have induced collective hallucination or heightened suggestibility.

3. **Hidden Human Intervention**  
   A scribe or priest could have written the words with some substance that only became visible under certain light (e.g., phosphorus compounds, lime wash reacting with moisture). When lit by torchlight, it would appear to suddenly glow.

4. **Symbolic Literary Device**  
   Some historians argue that the story is more theological parable than literal history. The "hand" represents divine judgment, not a physical phenomenon to be explained.

## Assistant

The “writing on the wall” in **Daniel 5** is a perfect case study of dialectical motion inside the Bible. If we trace it step by step, we can see how it starts as one kind of story, how it develops, and what it becomes as Scripture is remembered and reused.  

---

### 1. **Immediate Context – The Court Tale**
- **Thesis (human power and arrogance):** Belshazzar hosts a feast, flaunting his power and mocking God by drinking from the sacred vessels.  
- **Antithesis (divine interruption):** A mysterious hand writes words no one can read, breaking into the king’s supposed invincibility.  
- **Synthesis (judgment through interpretation):** Daniel, a Jewish exile, explains the words. Power shifts away from Babylon’s ruler and toward the God of Israel.  

The motion here is that the story negates the permanence of empire. It says: kings rise and fall, but divine justice interrupts their self-celebration.  

---

### 2. **In the Exilic Context – Survival of Israel**
For the Jewish people in exile, the story functioned as a reassurance. The empire that looks eternal is actually fragile. The “hand of God” writes judgment against Babylon, even while Israel seems powerless.  
- Dialectically, the story converts the **humiliation of exile** into a **lesson of endurance**. The vessels of the Temple, once lost, become instruments of God’s own condemnation of their captors.  

---

### 3. **In Later Jewish and Christian Memory**
The phrase “writing on the wall” becomes proverbial. It shifts from a single miracle into a general warning: the signs of collapse are visible, though not everyone can interpret them.  
- Here the dialectical motion is that a **theological vision** transforms into a **social proverb**. What was once about Belshazzar becomes a metaphor for every empire, every arrogance, every “end that is coming.”  

---

### 4. **Purpose in the Larger Arc of the Bible**
The story is not random—it sets up a moral pattern:  
- Human kingdoms are temporary.  
- God’s justice is eternal.  
- Only those aligned with truth can interpret history correctly.  

By the time the New Testament arrives, this same structure is echoed in Christ’s warnings about the coming judgment of Jerusalem and Rome. The parables of watchfulness, the warnings about signs in the heavens, the critique of rulers who exalt themselves—all of these are the New Testament’s development of the same dialectical motion.  

---

### 5. **Materialist Christian Purpose**
From a Materialist Christian lens, the story functions to train consciousness:  
- It teaches that **power always carries the seed of its own downfall**.  
- The oppressed do not need to destroy empire by might alone; they can endure, knowing that the very structure of empire contains the hand that will write its judgment.  
- Daniel is the interpreter—the revolutionary consciousness that makes sense of the signs of history.  

---

So the dialectical motion is:  
**Mockery of God (empire’s arrogance) → Interruption by judgment (the hand) → Interpretation by the oppressed (Daniel) → Collapse of empire → Proverb for all history.**  

The purpose is to arm future readers with the certainty that no matter how permanent oppression feels, its fate is already written.
