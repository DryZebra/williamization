### 1. **Mechanical Engineering – Structural Strength**

In components, grooves act as geometric discontinuities. They locally amplify stress compared to the rest of the structure—a concept known as the **stress concentration factor (Kₜ)**. For example, a simple hole can triple the local stress (Kₜ ≈ 3) compared to nominal stress levels citeturn0search22. In effect, grooves can make parts more prone to failure if not carefully designed.

---

### 2. **Blade Design – The Fuller Groove**

Grooves in blades, commonly referred to as *fullers*, actually **enhance performance**. By contouring material into a groove (instead of removing it), fullers reduce weight—sometimes by **20–35%**—while retaining much of the blade’s stiffness and overall strength, thanks to their clever geometry citeturn0search21. It’s similar in principle to why an I‑beam is so efficient structurally.

---

### 3. **Golfing – Wedge Groove Performance**

If you were talking about “groove” in the golf sense—like wedge grooves—it’s a whole different kind of “strength.” New, sharp grooves substantially enhance spin and shot control.

- **Spin increases up to 2,000 RPM** for a fresh wedge compared to one used for 125+ rounds citeturn0search7turn0search3.
- Specifically, fresh wedges can deliver around **8,500 RPM**, while those worn down through 125 rounds might only deliver **6,500 RPM** citeturn0search3.
- On rollout: fresh wedges result in **10 feet** of roll, whereas a heavily used wedge might roll out to **24 feet**—more than double citeturn0search3.
- Titleist recommends inspecting your wedges for groove wear **every 60–75 rounds** citeturn0search7turn0search3.
