## Assistant

*Materialist Christianity* is a work of philosophy and theology that reinterprets Christianity through a dialectical materialist lens. It argues that Christianity did not invent moral truth but discovered it—uncovering universal, material laws of human moral development through history. The book rejects the false divide between atheism and Christianity, showing that secular humanism is, in essence, Christianity without its metaphysical claim, and that both emerge from the same historical process.  

The author traces the evolution of biblical morality as a living reflection of humanity’s social consciousness, revealing how Scripture’s teachings arose from the material struggles of real communities. Christianity is presented not as an arbitrary set of beliefs but as the historical culmination of humanity’s search for a moral order capable of sustaining life across generations. This perspective treats the Bible as a record of moral discovery, forged in the crucible of oppression, liberation, betrayal, and renewal.  

Through this lens, familiar teachings—such as “know them by their fruits” or the parable of the leaven—become material truths about the social process: the “fruit” is the tangible result of a moral system, while the “leaven” is the transformative process that shapes it. The book emphasizes that morality can only be judged in motion, by its capacity to sustain human dignity and cooperation over time.  

*Materialist Christianity* also critiques purely metaphysical faith, which can lead to passivity in the face of injustice, and purely secular morality, which risks severing itself from the historical wisdom of religion. Instead, it calls for a unity of moral action and historical consciousness—a faith that stands on the real, material truths discovered by generations past, while refusing to deny the lived process that produced them.  

By reintegrating atheists into the Christian moral tradition without requiring supernatural belief, the book proposes a framework where truth, virtue, and humanity remain in balance. It is both a challenge and an invitation: to see the Kingdom of God not as an otherworldly hope, but as the concrete, communal work of building a just society here and now—an inheritance not from divine decree, but from the long, unfinished labor of human history.
