# Israel dialing format

## Assistant

That’s right—Israel (like several other countries) doesn’t normally use the **“+” symbol** in phone numbers the way we do.  

Instead, people there dial **“00”** (or sometimes **“+” if the phone allows it). For example:  

- If you’re in Israel and want to call the U.S. number **+1-212-555-1234**, you would dial:  
  **00-1-212-555-1234**  

On most modern cell phones, the “+” still works as an international prefix, but officially Israel’s international dialing code is **00**.

Ah got it — you mean in **math notation**, not phone numbers.  

In Israel, math is usually written using **Hebrew notation** conventions. The **“+” symbol does exist and is understood**, but in elementary school especially, you’ll often see the **Hebrew letter “וּ” (vav with dagesh, pronounced “u”)** or even the word **"פלוס" (plus, written out in Hebrew)** used instead of the “+” symbol.  

So for example:  
- Instead of writing `2 + 3`, a workbook for younger students might show `2 וּ 3`.  
- Higher levels of math (high school, university) usually switch back to the standard international `+`.  

It’s kind of like how in some countries multiplication is shown with `×` for kids but `·` or parentheses later on.

## Assistant

It feels universal because most of us grow up with it, but actually math notation has **never been fully standardized across languages or history**.  

A couple examples:  

- **Addition**: as I mentioned, in Israel you’ll sometimes see וּ (vav with dagesh) or the Hebrew word *פלוס* instead of `+`.  
- **Multiplication**: English speakers learn `×`, but in higher math it becomes `·` or parentheses. In France they often use `x` in elementary school, which can confuse learners later.  
- **Division**: Americans use `÷`, but much of the world just writes fractions or `/`.  
- **Decimals**: In the U.S. it’s `3.14`, but in much of Europe it’s `3,14`.  
- **Square roots**: The radical √ is common now, but older texts sometimes just wrote “rq” or used overlines.  
- **Equality**: The `=` sign itself was invented in 1557 by Robert Recorde; before that people wrote phrases like “is equal to.”  

So `+` is *almost* universal now, but even that took centuries to spread—and there are still small variations, especially in early education.
