# A Dialectic of Mammon, Money, and the Church

1) **Jesus names the master**  
The dialectic begins where Christ begins: not with a theory of finance, but with a rival lord. “You cannot serve God and Mammon.” He does not say “money and God,” because Mammon is more than coin; it is the social power of accumulation—the universal claim that all goods, all time, all bodies must pass through exchange to count. Skeptics imagine chaos without money because they only know price signals. These are *thick* signals. Money is a *thin* signal that was necessary when strangers traded across distance without trust. The Church’s task is to thicken signals until price is unnecessary for most life. That is not a leap to paradise; it is an accretion of working habits that make price superfluous in practice and finally obsolete in scope.

13) **The material inevitability**  
As private accumulation is refused, three material shifts occur:  
- **Liquidity collapses into locality.** Value stops sloshing toward distant balance sheets and stays where it was earned, because spending becomes skilled caregiving, not abstract purchasing.  
- **Reproduction costs fall.** Shared tools, open designs, pooled maintenance erase markups; the same resources reproduce more life.  
- **Trust compounds faster than interest.** Each fulfilled obligation lowers future coordination cost. Compounded across years, trust outgrows finance. When the cost of doing things together falls beneath the “price advantage” of markets, the market loses its edge and then its necessity.

14) **Why this is not utopia**  
Utopia abolishes tragedy. The Church abolishes private mastery. Sickness remains; scarcity happens; planning fails; sin returns; we repent and re-knit. The difference is not a fantasy of painless history; it is the stubborn refusal to let scarcity be monetized into dominion. The body absorbs shocks through love; it does not auction relief to the highest donor.

15) **How money finally dies**  
Money dies exactly as idols die: not by argument, but by starvation and replacement. We starve it by refusing to store command in numbers. We replace it by binding our days to one another through vows, rosters, and gifts. At first, only within the Church; then in neighborhoods that learn the habit; then in trades that prefer the dignity of craft over the drama of price; then in cities that keep their surplus human. The universal commodity form withers because it has nothing left to universalize: when each gives and receives in kind, equivalence is unnecessary.

Work with your hands. Keep your books open. Zero your surplus on a rhythm. Translate every dollar into bodies and tools. Refuse profit among brethren. Teach trades as diaconal service. When you are praised for “financial prudence,” make sure the praise is for *speed of generosity*, not height of reserve. If you must hold a reserve for law or timing, set the date it will die and keep that date holy.

17) **Name the master, choose the Lord**  
The neutral-money thesis is not a harmless opinion; it is Mammon’s ventriloquism. Jesus gave you the test: masters do not share. If money can command your calendar, your plans, your friendships, your courage, it is not neutral. The Church’s confession is simple: *We will touch it only to translate it, we will earn only to dissolve it, we will save only to schedule its death into the lives of the brethren.* As this habit hardens into joy, labor becomes life’s prime want, exchange loses its throne, and money—having been stripped of breath, liturgy, and fear—finally returns to what it always pretended to be: metal, paper, digits, nothing.
