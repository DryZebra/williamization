Got it — I’ll talk straight, not PR-mode.

What you’re bumping into isn’t a small quirk; it’s one of the oldest tension-points in Christian history. Catholics are very aware of the charge of “polytheism,” but the way they understand what they’re doing is structurally different from what you’re describing, even though from the outside it looks like old-school paganism with a Christian coat of paint. Here’s the real breakdown:

---

### 1. **Intercession vs. Worship**

When Catholics say they “ask Mary to pray for us,” they’re claiming it’s the same thing as you asking your pastor or grandmother to pray for you. You don’t think you’re worshiping your grandmother by saying “please pray for me.”  
The Catholic idea of the *Communion of Saints* extends that logic to the dead — especially the righteous dead — because they believe those people are alive in Christ (Luke 20:38) and can still pray.

From their point of view:  
- **Worship** = adoration (latria) → God alone.  
- **Veneration** = honor (dulia) → saints.  
- **Special veneration** = hyperdulia → Mary (unique because of her role in salvation history).  

They are genuinely convinced these are different “categories” of action. Protestants often don’t buy that the line is real — but Catholics think it’s as clear as the difference between loving your wife and worshiping God.

---

### 2. **Monotheism with “Many Friends”**

Catholicism isn’t claiming saints are divine beings with independent power. They’re claiming saints *participate* in God’s power as members of His Body. The saints’ “specialties” (Anthony for lost things, Jude for hopeless causes, etc.) aren’t supposed to be cosmic jurisdictions like Greek gods; they’re more like spiritual “mentors” whose lives are exemplary in that area, so people feel a personal resonance when asking for their prayers.

So where paganism has multiple autonomous gods with competing wills, Catholicism claims:  
- **One divine will** (God).  
- **Many intercessors** (saints) who can only act because God permits it.  

From inside their system, that preserves monotheism.

---

### 3. **Why It Feels Pagan Anyway**

The human mind gravitates toward concrete, relational figures. In practice, Catholic popular devotion *does* look like mini-gods: statues, candles, niches, feast days, “patronages.” The Church tries to police the language (no worship of saints, only veneration), but the folk practice often blurs the line. Historically, this was deliberate: early Christians *repurposed* the pagan instinct for local gods and household patrons by redirecting it toward martyrs and saints. It was a way to baptize the culture rather than annihilate it.

So you’re not imagining things. Structurally, Catholicism absorbed the social function of polytheism and reinterpreted it as “family of saints under one God.”

---

### 4. **What’s Really Going On**

If you strip away the theological terms, Catholicism is a **relationship-heavy, incarnational religion**. It doesn’t want people relating to an abstract deity alone; it wants them to experience a whole communion of living examples, rituals, places, and persons. That’s why it feels earthy and plural.  
Protestantism tends to strip all that down to “God and the individual believer.” Catholicism says that’s too thin — you need the cloud of witnesses and sacramental life.  

This difference explains why Catholics can sincerely call themselves monotheists while Protestants look at the same behavior and see paganism.

---

### 5. **Bottom Line**

- **Catholics’ claim:** We do not worship Mary or the saints; we only ask them to pray for us. God alone is God.  
- **Protestant perception:** This walks and talks like polytheism.  
- **Historical reality:** Catholicism repurposed the polytheistic instinct into a monotheistic framework. Whether you accept the distinction depends on whether you think “veneration” vs. “worship” is a real category or just word games.
