You’re right that Jesus Himself never said “money is evil.” He consistently spoke of *love of money* and the way it distorts the heart. The famous line “the love of money is the root of all evil” comes not from Jesus but from **1 Timothy 6:10**. What Jesus said was closer to: *“You cannot serve God and mammon”* (Matthew 6:24). In other words, He framed wealth as a rival master, not inherently evil but a power that competes for loyalty.  

As for James—his language is sharper. In **James 5:1–6**, he condemns the rich who hoard wealth, exploit workers, and live in luxury while others suffer. He doesn’t call “money itself” evil, but he treats wealth, once accumulated at the expense of others, as a force crying out against the oppressor. So James is not abstract—he sees money as inseparable from the *relations of exploitation* behind it.  

So the Materialist Christian response would be:  

- Money itself is like bread—neutral until it enters human use.  
- But unlike bread, money is *structurally designed* to become mammon. It concentrates, it accumulates, it becomes a rival master.  
- Jesus named that rival master plainly: you will either serve God or serve Mammon.  
- James takes the next step and shows what serving Mammon looks like: hoarding, exploitation, injustice.

1. Telos and use-value. Everything in creation has a use that completes it: bread feeds, iron builds, water quenches. Those objects achieve fulfillment by being put to their purpose. Money is different: it’s a symbol, an accounting relation, a claim on other things. It does nothing by itself. Its “purpose” is purely relational and recursive — to be exchanged for other relations that increase itself. That structural emptiness is what makes it liturgically dangerous: it always points outward to more of itself.

2. Self-expansion as idolatry. Capital’s driving imperative — make more capital — is not a neutral technical rule, it’s an ethic. Unlike other tools, money’s ideal state is to multiply, indefinitely. Nothing in nature or human life requires infinite multiplication. So when money becomes your master it becomes an idol with an impossible, insatiable goal. Jesus’ phrase “you cannot serve God and Mammon” names that polarity: one master creates life, the other devours it for its own sake (Matt. 6:24). Capital accumulation doesn’t care whether children are fed or workers live in dignity; it cares whether the balance sheet grows.
