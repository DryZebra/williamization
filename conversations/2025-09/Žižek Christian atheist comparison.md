# 1) Framing the Dispute: Terms, Stakes, Audiences

Here is the choice in plain speech. Two programs, both using Christian language, both speaking to people who feel the floor shift under them. One program is Christian atheism. The other is Materialist Christianity. They are not cousins, they are not two doors into the same room. Christian atheism is a negative theology, organized around the absence of God. Materialist Christianity is a positive, work-tested pattern, organized around motion, behavior, and repeatable consequences. If you are a believer, or an atheist, or somewhere between, the test is not who you say you are, the test is what the form makes you do, and whether it works under pressure.

Terms first, without fog. “Christian atheism,” as Žižek uses it, says Christianity reveals the death of the Big Other. The cross exposes the collapse of a metaphysical guarantor, so responsibility sits on us, alone, and the Spirit becomes a name for our communal bond after the guarantee is gone. “Materialist Christianity,” as I mean it, says Christianity is the historical discovery of a moral pattern that survives collapse. Real is what exerts force. Truth is what keeps moving after the wound. The Bible is not a code from above, it is the record of people learning what keeps a community alive, through betrayal, through empire, through fire, and still walking. If you need a single sentence to hold the difference, use this. Christian atheism organizes around a negation. Materialist Christianity organizes around a method.

Why this matters now, and not only in seminar rooms. Because the old guarantees are gone in people’s daily lives, not just in books. Because you can feel the loss of the Big Other when your plant closes, when your boss cuts the crew, when your church bleeds families, when your body fails and the bill still comes due. Because millions live inside that gap between what the world promised and what the world delivers. You do not need a clever paradox in that place. You need a rule of life that yields bread, and trust, and forgiveness, and a way to keep working together after someone fails. A rule you can teach to your kid. A rule you can carry into a union meeting, or a kitchen, or a hospital hallway at two in the morning. A rule that does not depend on whether you can force your heart to believe a metaphysical sentence today.

There is an agreement to honor before we mark the split. Žižek is right that Christianity sits at the center of modern responsibility. He is right that the cross tears open the idea of a cosmic insurance plan. He is right that the weight falls back onto human shoulders, that community matters, that we must act without waiting for a thunderclap. He is right that Christianity, with its hard line between Creator and creation, made atheism thinkable in the first place, because a world de-godded is a world that can be examined, rebuilt, governed, and judged by its fruits. He is right about the danger of sentimental spirituality that hides from the cost of action. If you only meet him in that space, he feels like an ally.

But here is the rupture we cannot smooth over. He treats the negation as the center. He builds his account around the death, around the absence, around the collapse of the guarantee, and then he names the Spirit as the love that remains in the room. The absence does all the heavy lifting. The community is what is left over. That is elegant, and it helps some people let go of childish belief, but it still keeps metaphysics in charge. God is the main character even when God is written out of the script. The negative still commands the frame. People are organized by a conclusion about heaven.

Materialist Christianity refuses that frame. We begin with reality as consequence. We begin with behavior that stabilizes dignity. We begin with motion that survives rupture. We audit by fruits. If a form organizes bread on a table, if it reconciles enemies into co-workers, if it disciplines wealth toward care, if it teaches a liar to tell the truth and then to keep telling it when it costs him, if it keeps going when the structure breaks, then it is real, whether or not anyone can settle the argument about what sits beyond the sky. If a form feels holy but dies as soon as it is betrayed, it was not the thing we are talking about. The cross is not a metaphysical event that proves absence.
