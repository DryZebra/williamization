# Founding Fathers on Religion

## User

Where does the founding fathers say that the constitution basically required a good and religious people? The documents themselves never contain any mention of God, the famous "we forgot." But did they talk to each other about Christianity? Or write about it in general?

## Assistant



## Assistant

You’re right that the *Constitution itself* is a godless document—something even the framers’ contemporaries noticed. Unlike state constitutions of the time, it does not invoke God, Providence, or Christianity. That omission was deliberate: the federal framework was meant to be secular in form, binding together states with different religious establishments and tolerances.  

But outside of the official text, the men who wrote and ratified it were not silent about religion. They constantly remarked—sometimes in public speeches, sometimes in private letters—that republican government could only function if the people themselves were moral, and in their language, “morality” was almost always tied to religion, especially Christianity. A few key examples:

---

**1. George Washington (Farewell Address, 1796):**  
“Of all the dispositions and habits which lead to political prosperity, religion and morality are indispensable supports. … Reason and experience both forbid us to expect that national morality can prevail in exclusion of religious principle.”  

Washington is clear: you can’t maintain liberty without a people trained in morality, and for him religion was the schoolhouse of morality.

---

**2. John Adams (Letter to Massachusetts militia, 1798):**  
“Our Constitution was made only for a moral and religious People. It is wholly inadequate to the government of any other.”  

This is probably the most famous and blunt statement of the point you’re raising. Adams meant that because the Constitution limits government power, it requires the people to govern themselves by internal discipline. Without morality rooted in religion, the system collapses.

---

**3. Benjamin Franklin (Constitutional Convention, June 28, 1787):**  
“I have lived, Sir, a long time, and the longer I live, the more convincing proofs I see of this truth—that God governs in the affairs of men. … except the Lord build the House, they labour in vain that build it.”  

Franklin proposed prayers at the Convention (though they never happened formally). Even the old rationalist deist was appealing to Providence.

---

**4. Gouverneur Morris, James Madison, and others:**  
Privately, many framers leaned deist or skeptical. Madison especially was wary of clerical influence and opposed religious establishments. But even he argued in *Federalist 55* that the Constitution presumes a people with enough virtue to use liberty wisely:  
“As there is a degree of depravity in mankind which requires a certain degree of circumspection and distrust, so there are other qualities in human nature which justify a certain portion of esteem and confidence. Republican government presupposes the existence of these qualities in a higher degree than any other form.”  

He doesn’t say “Christianity” here, but in the culture of the time those “qualities” were assumed to be formed by religion.

---

So here’s the dialectic:  
- **The Constitution itself** is secular, designed that way.  
- **The framers in speech and correspondence** repeatedly said republican liberty requires morality, and morality requires religion.  
- In practice, they assumed Christianity (in its broad, Protestant sense) was the school of virtue that would maintain the republic.  

They didn’t put God in the Constitution not because they rejected Him, but because they were creating a *federal framework* that sat above denominations. But in their own words, without a religious and moral citizenry, the whole project falls apart.
