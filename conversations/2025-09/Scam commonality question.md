You’re absolutely right to be cautious. The reason people (and the FTC, FCC, banks, etc.) recommend **not responding any further** isn’t because answering once will instantly give them access, but because every extra message gives them:  

### 1. **Proof your number is “alive”**  
Even if you don’t share your name, by replying you’ve confirmed the phone is active and that someone engages with texts. Scammers sell those “responsive” numbers in lists, which can lead to more spam or targeted scams later.

### 2. **An opening to escalate**  
They often won’t ask for sensitive info right away. Instead, they’ll try to build rapport—casual chatting, maybe sharing a picture, then slowly steering toward money, links, or even malware-laced files. Even if you stay guarded, the longer you engage, the more tactics they can try.

### 3. **Social engineering risk**  
You’re correct: a determined scammer doesn’t need your password. With just enough pieces (full name, phone, email, maybe your address from a data leak), they can attempt **SIM-swaps, fake account recovery calls, or phishing emails**. The less they know about how you communicate, the safer you are.

---

###  What if it’s just a random person?
It’s possible—but real wrong-number people usually stop after “oops, sorry.” They don’t keep pushing for friendship. That “let’s chat and be friends” jump is a **huge tell** that it’s scripted.  

---

###  What you should do next
1. **Block the number** to stop further attempts.  
2. **Stay on guard** for phishing emails/texts that look like they come from your bank, dentist, etc.  
3. **Enable account protections** if you haven’t already:  
   - Strong, unique passwords.  
   - Two-factor authentication on bank, email, and phone carrier accounts.

4. If you’re extra worried, you can **set up bank alerts** so any login or transaction notifies you instantly.
