# Electricians before CNC

## User

How did electricians work before CNC machinists?

## Assistant



## Assistant

Before CNC machinists existed (really before the 1950s–70s when CNC became common), electricians worked the same way most of the trade still does today: by hand. CNC machining didn’t directly change how electricians pull wire, terminate panels, or bend conduit—but it did change how some of their tools and prefabricated parts are made.  

Here’s the breakdown:

---

### **Hand Tools and Manual Work**
- **Conduit bending** was done entirely with hickeys (manual bending tools), mechanical benders, and heat for PVC. Every bend was measured, marked, and bent by hand.
- **Panels and enclosures** were drilled, punched, and filed by hand. Greenlee knockout punches (invented in the 1930s) let electricians make precise holes for conduit before CNC punch presses existed.
- **Wire pulling** was always manual, using fish tape, soap, and muscle. Mechanical pullers existed, but nothing computer-guided.
- **Terminations** were hand-crimped or soldered long before CNC-produced lugs and precision dies.

---

### **Fabrication and Prefab**
- Before CNC, if an electrician needed a custom bus bar, enclosure, or bracket, it was:
  - Cut with a saw or torch.
  - Drilled with a drill press.
  - Filed to fit by hand.
  - Welded or bolted together.
- Shops had machinists, but they used manual lathes, mills, and punches. Electricians depended on these machinists for parts, but nothing was automated.

---

### **Blueprints and Layout**
- Layouts were done with tape measures, plumb bobs, and chalk lines—no CAD/CAM integration.
- Electricians read hand-drawn blueprints, then transferred them to the job site manually.

---

### **Impact of CNC Later On**
- CNC machinists made it faster to prefab panels, bus ducts, cable trays, and custom brackets.
- It reduced the need for electricians to do as much filing, drilling, or fitting on-site—parts arrived pre-cut and ready.
- But **the core electrical work stayed the same**: pulling, terminating, testing, and energizing systems.

---

⚡ In short: electricians always worked primarily by hand with simple mechanical aids. CNC didn’t replace electrician skills—it just industrialized the shop side of the trade (custom enclosures, bus bars, prefab). The field work itself hasn’t fundamentally changed.

How was wire made before machinists?

## Assistant

Good question — wire is actually one of the oldest mass-produced industrial products, and it long predates machinists in the modern sense. Here’s the historical flow:  

---

### **Antiquity – Medieval**
- **Copper & bronze wire** was made as early as 2000 BC. Archaeologists have found Egyptian, Mesopotamian, and Roman wire jewelry.  
- The technique was simple: a small rod of soft metal was **hammered into a strip** and then **drawn through holes in stone or iron plates** (called *drawplates*).  
- This didn’t require machinists, just blacksmiths or goldsmiths. The drawplate itself was forged by hand.  

---

### **Renaissance – Early Industry (1200s–1700s)**
- In Europe, **water-powered wire drawing mills** appeared in the 1300s. The water wheel pulled metal through drawplates, allowing much longer lengths of consistent wire.  
- Wire makers were often their own guild (e.g., the Nürnberg *Drahtzieher* in Germany).  
- Iron, brass, and copper wire became standard for chainmail, jewelry, and early electrical experiments.  

---

### **Industrial Revolution (1700s–1800s)**
- Steelmaking and rolling mills made rods uniform before drawing.  
- Wire drawing was still essentially manual: a worker pulled the rod through progressively smaller holes until it reached the desired gauge.  
- By the mid-1800s, steam power and then electric motors mechanized the pulling process, increasing speed and consistency.  

---

### **Before “machinists” as we think of them**
- “Machinists” as a trade really emerges in the late 1800s with precision lathes, mills, and interchangeable parts.  
- **Wire, though, never required a machinist.** The process was blacksmithing + specialized millwork, not precision machining.  
- The drawplates and rollers were made by smiths or later by industrial foundries, not by bench machinists.  

---

### **Electrical Age (late 1800s onward)**
- As electricity spread, copper wire demand exploded.  
- Factories used large rolling mills and multi-stage drawbenches powered by steam, then electricity.  
- Insulation (gutta-percha, then rubber, later plastics) was applied after drawing.  

---

⚡ **So the bottom line:**  
Wire was made long before machinists existed as a craft. It came from hammering and drawing metal through holes, first by hand, then with water wheels, then with steam and electric motors. Machinists later made the tools *better* (precision dies, automated machines), but the essential technique — drawing metal thinner by force — is ancient.

---

### **Electricians**
- The **electrician trade** is much younger. It doesn’t really exist until **the late 1800s**, after the invention of the telegraph (1840s), telephone (1870s), and electric power distribution (1880s).

- The first organized electrician unions in the U.S. date to the 1890s (IBEW founded 1891).  

---

### **So Who Came First?**
- **Machinists predate electricians by nearly a century.**  
- Machinists emerged as a skilled trade with the Industrial Revolution (~1700s–early 1800s).  
- Electricians emerged only after electricity became a practical technology (~1880s onward).  

---

⚡ **In short:** Machinists came first. Electricians didn’t exist as a distinct craft until industry actually needed electrical systems installed and maintained.
