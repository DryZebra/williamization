## Assistant

That sermon is probably leaning on Paul’s famous words in **1 Corinthians 13**, where he says you can have all gifts, knowledge, even give your body to be burned—but if you have not love, it profits nothing. The danger, as you caught, is when “love” is turned into an abstraction or a feeling instead of something real.  

Paul didn’t mean love as sentiment floating in the air. He meant love as *motion*. In his letters, love is patient, kind, bears burdens, rejoices with truth, does not envy, does not puff up (1 Cor 13:4–7). In other words, love is measured *through what it does in the world*. It is not opposed to deeds—it is the animating spirit of deeds.  

In a materialist Christian sense, you can think of it like this: Paul wasn’t saying the church is judged by invisible “vibes.” He was saying the measure is whether their deeds actually arise from love—whether their works *move toward the wounded, forgive betrayal, continue after rupture*【13:18†materialist_christianity_clean.docx†L1-L11】. Without that, good works become spectacle or power plays. With it, even small acts—feeding one person, forgiving one hurt—become real continuity of Christ.  

So when someone asks, “how do you measure love?” the answer is: by whether it survives contradiction. Does it keep moving when the structure breaks? Does it bless when strength runs out? That is love. It isn’t measurable by tally, but by endurance. By what remains when everything else collapses .  

Paul’s point was not that love replaces works, but that works without love rot into nothing. Love is the difference between a ritual and resurrection. Between an act that ends, and an act that carries.
