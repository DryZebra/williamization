### Where this misses the mark  
They’re mistaking the **myth of capitalism** for its reality:  
1. **Barriers to entry grow with maturity.**  
   In early capitalism, small firms could out-innovate giants. In late-stage capitalism, huge firms buy or kill competitors before they can threaten them. Apple today is not in Blackberry’s shoes—it’s closer to a monopoly with enough cash to acquire or suppress rivals.  

2. **Innovation becomes financialized.**  
   Investment isn’t guided toward the *best* ideas but toward the safest returns. That means new ideas often get buried unless they can be monopolized.  

3. **Creative destruction narrows.**  
   Early on, it’s real: new industries replace old. Later, destruction still happens—but it’s not “creative.” Think of mass layoffs, asset stripping, or buying startups just to shelve their products. That’s destruction without innovation.

That’s actually a really old Christian instinct you’re voicing, and it gets right to the heart of the Sabbath debate.  

### Early Church Practice  
- **Sabbath (7th day / Saturday):** In Judaism, the day of rest was commanded in the Ten Commandments. Early Jewish Christians continued keeping Sabbath.  
- **Lord’s Day (1st day / Sunday):** The resurrection was on the first day of the week. Very early on (2nd century and maybe earlier), Christians began gathering on Sunday for worship and Eucharist. This was not called “Sabbath” at first — it was “the Lord’s Day.”  

### Why the Split Happened  
- Over time, especially as Christianity became predominantly Gentile, Sunday worship became the primary Christian identity marker, separating believers from Jewish Sabbath-keeping.  
- By the 4th century (Council of Laodicea), the church explicitly moved Christians away from the 7th-day Sabbath, teaching that the Lord’s Day was the day of rest and worship.  

### Theological Angle  
- Some groups (like Seventh-day Adventists) argue Sabbath never changed, so it *must* be observed on Saturday.  
- Most other Christians say the **resurrection “fulfilled” the Sabbath** — that Christ’s rest in the tomb on Saturday and His rising on Sunday transformed the old command into something new.
