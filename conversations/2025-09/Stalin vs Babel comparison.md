Good question. Let’s line it up clearly against Stalin’s *Marxism and Problems of Linguistics* (1950) and your concern about identity politics.

---

### What Stalin’s book does
In *Marxism and Problems of Linguistics*, Stalin is intervening in a debate within Soviet academia. His main points are:
- Language is **not** a superstructure but a tool of communication that persists across class societies.  
- Unlike law, religion, or ideology, language doesn’t disappear with the overthrow of a ruling class.  
- He rejects the idea that a “proletarian language” or “bourgeois language” can exist.  
- Thus, Marxists should treat language as a **neutral instrument** that serves different classes in different ways, not as a class-bound ideology.

In short: Stalin is defending materialist clarity about what language is, preventing it from being muddled with idealist or class-essentialist interpretations.

---

### What Kuang’s *Babel* does
From the blurb and what I know of the novel:
- It’s a **historical fantasy**, not theory.  
- Language and translation are made into a literal tool of power (silver-working magic).  
- The British Empire’s domination is sustained not only by violence and capital, but by control of words and meanings.  
- The story follows Robin, a Chinese orphan raised in England, caught between the benefits of Oxford and the betrayal of empire.  
- The core tension is whether empire can be resisted from within academic institutions (*reform vs. revolution*).  

So unlike Stalin, who clarifies that language is *not* inherently tied to class, Kuang deliberately dramatizes language as a **weapon of class and empire**—a narrative device to expose colonial power.

---

### Is it “liberal identity politics”?
Not exactly. Kuang is interested in:
- **Colonialism and resistance**: the way empire uses language, institutions, and translation to extract and dominate.  
- **Student revolution vs. reformism**: whether compromise is betrayal.  
- **Identity conflict**: Robin is Chinese but raised in Britain, torn between two worlds.  

That *does* mean there’s a lot of racial/colonial identity exploration, but it’s not the shallow “representation talk” of liberal politics. It’s framed inside a broader material power struggle (silver, empire, opium wars). The book does circle back to a key Marxist question: can the oppressed transform imperial institutions, or must they break them?

---

### Direct comparison
- **Stalin**: Language is neutral infrastructure, not a weapon in itself.  
- **Kuang**: Language (via magic) is the weapon of empire.  
- **Overlap**: Both works treat language as central to power and survival, but one grounds it in materialist science, the other in allegorical fantasy.  
- **Divergence**: Stalin strips away mystification; Kuang leans into it as narrative.  

---

👉 If you’re reading it expecting a Marxist manual, you’ll be disappointed. If you read it as a **fantasy critique of empire through the metaphor of language**, it could still be stimulating—though yes, it leans toward identity-framed dilemmas rather than Stalin’s strict materialism.
