This is a really important and often misunderstood question. Let me give you the **historical, biblical, and material context** for why the Israelites were commanded to drive out (and sometimes kill) the Canaanites, and why people today often frame it as "genocide."

---

## 1. The Biblical Text
The clearest commands are in **Deuteronomy 7** and **Deuteronomy 20**, where Israel is told to "completely destroy" the Canaanite nations. The word often translated as *utterly destroy* is **ḥerem** (חֵרֶם), which means something like "devote to destruction"—a ritual term of judgment, not just a military strategy.

At the same time, other passages (like **Deuteronomy 10:18–19** and **Exodus 22:21**) stress kindness to foreigners and protection of resident aliens. This creates a tension: Israel is not commanded to kill *all foreigners*, but specifically certain nations in a certain land.

---

## 2. Historical and Material Context
- **Canaanite practices**: Archaeology and biblical texts describe Canaanite religion as involving child sacrifice, temple prostitution, and extreme forms of social exploitation. From Israel’s perspective, these practices represented corruption that would destroy their fragile new society.
- **Small tribal context**: The Israelites were not an empire with armies marching across the world. They were a loose confederation of tribes, fresh out of slavery, trying to establish a homeland. The "wars" described are local tribal struggles over territory.
- **Exaggerated war language**: Ancient Near Eastern texts often used hyperbolic language of "total destruction," even when the actual result was a partial displacement or subjugation. For example, Israel is said to have "destroyed all" the Canaanites, but Judges shows many Canaanites still living in the land.

---

## 3. Theological and Narrative Function
- **Separation from idolatry**: The command wasn’t framed as racial extermination, but as removal of practices that would turn Israel away from their God. Deuteronomy explicitly says, "It is not because of your righteousness, but because of their wickedness" (Deut. 9:5).
- **Covenant purity**: Israel was seen as a holy nation with a unique vocation. Mixing with the Canaanites wasn’t framed as cultural diversity but as adopting destructive practices that would unravel their society.
- **Judgment, not expansion**: Unlike empires, Israel wasn’t commanded to conquer the whole world—only the land of Canaan. The wars are framed as divine judgment on specific nations, not general conquest.

---

## 4. Why People Call It Genocide
Modern readers, looking back through the lens of colonialism, see commands like "kill them all" and read it as equivalent to ethnic cleansing. But in its context:
- It wasn’t global domination, but a survival struggle of a small people.
- The commands were *conditional* on specific nations in a specific time, not an eternal law.
- Even within the Bible, there is evidence that the commands were not carried out literally (since Canaanites remained in the land).

## 5. A Materialist Christian Reading
