# Fishmen Allegory Interpretation

## User

Fishmen in One piece. Jews or African Americans?

## Assistant



## Assistant

Fishmen in *One Piece* are most directly an allegory for **racism and systemic discrimination**—and while their story can reflect **aspects** of both the **Black** and **Jewish** experience (among others), they are **not a 1-to-1 representation** of either. Let’s break it down dialectically:

---

### 🔹 **How They Reflect the African American Experience**
- **Slavery**: Fishmen were historically enslaved by humans—echoing the transatlantic slave trade and the dehumanization of Black people.
- **Segregation and fear**: They live underwater, often excluded from human society. There’s a clear sense of being "the other."
- **Anger and radicalization**: Characters like Arlong express bitterness and a desire to flip the hierarchy, mirroring the rise of radical Black nationalism in response to centuries of oppression.
- **Intergenerational trauma**: Jinbe and Fisher Tiger’s arcs explore how trauma shapes communities and how forgiveness or vengeance can define the path forward.

---

### 🔹 **How They Reflect the Jewish Experience**
- **Diaspora and exile**: Fishmen aren’t just undersea natives—they were displaced and feared, much like Jews historically have been.
- **Misunderstood and scapegoated**: Humans often demonize Fishmen without knowing them, and myths spread about their nature—mirroring centuries of antisemitic blood libel and paranoia.
- **Tensions between integration and separatism**: There’s a constant tension between Fishmen who want peace with humans (like Otohime) and those who want separation or domination, reflecting real debates within Jewish communities across history about assimilation vs. maintaining identity.

---

### 🔹 **Oda’s Likely Intent**
Eiichiro Oda has not said explicitly, but it’s clear he **crafted Fishmen as a universal metaphor for the oppressed**. Their experience can resonate with:
- Black Americans
- Jewish people
- Immigrants
- Refugees
- Any group historically “othered” and abused

It’s not **“which group are they?”**—it’s **“how does this structure reflect real-world systems of oppression?”**

---

### 🔸 Conclusion:
**Fishmen are neither “the Jews” nor “Black people.”** They’re a *symbolic composite* of oppressed peoples—especially reflecting the **Black experience in America** more closely in terms of slavery, systemic racism, and liberation struggle. But elements of the **Jewish historical arc**—diaspora, mythologized fear, and debates over identity—are present too.

Oda didn’t create a direct allegory. He created a dialectic.
