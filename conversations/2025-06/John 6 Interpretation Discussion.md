## Assistant

To reach someone like your Catholic interlocutor—someone who takes *John 6* literally as proof of transubstantiation (the bread and wine becoming the literal flesh and blood of Christ)—you need to approach not by dismantling the *literalism* outright, but by exposing the *motion* and *context* of the text. Catholics aren't wrong that Jesus is saying something profound and embodied here. But the idea that He meant a digestive miracle ignores the actual thread of the passage.

Here’s how you might approach it:

---

**1. Begin with shared reverence.**  
Say something like:  
> “I agree this is one of the most powerful, radical teachings Jesus ever gave. He isn’t talking about a vague metaphor. He’s demanding something total—something visceral.”

This keeps the conversation centered on the *seriousness* of the command, not on whether it’s “just symbolic.”

---

**2. Contextualize the outrage.**  
Point out:  
> “The Jews weren’t confused. They were outraged. They *understood* exactly what He was saying: that He was the new covenant, that He would be torn, consumed, and that *they would participate* in it. The ‘eating’ is not digestion—it’s *internalization* of His suffering and resurrection.”

This helps shift the attention from the stomach to the soul—not abstractly, but bodily, ethically.

---

**3. Return to Scripture: John 6:63.**  
> “Jesus Himself clarifies later in the same chapter: ‘It is the Spirit that gives life, the flesh profits nothing. The words that I speak unto you, they are spirit, and they are life.’”

This verse is often under-emphasized. It doesn’t undo what He said—it *locates* it. He is talking about something spiritual and life-giving, but *not magic*. Not a digestive ritual. The Catholics take “This is my body” as physical, but Jesus calls His own words *Spirit*.

---

**4. Argue from the Book of Acts and early practice.**  
> “If transubstantiation was a known miracle, why do we see zero emphasis on the *moment* of transformation in the early Church? Why no reaction from outsiders to a supposed cannibalistic practice? Why isn’t Paul’s warning in 1 Corinthians 11 about failing to discern *literal* flesh and blood?”

The answer is: the early Church understood this as deeply real, but not material in the digestive sense. The power of the Eucharist was in union and remembrance, not magic.

---

**5. Final blow: Reverse the claim.**  
Ask them:  
> “If Jesus wanted us to literally eat His flesh, and drink His blood, in a digestive miracle... what would *not* be sufficient to teach that? Would He not say ‘do this in remembrance of me’? Would He not say ‘this bread is my body’? Would He not let Himself be broken like bread, poured out like wine?”

Then pause.

> “But if we accept that the real meaning of this is about covenant, memory, mutual indwelling—*flesh as action*, not tissue—then we can obey Him without pretending that a priest works a digestive miracle.”

---

You’re not trying to win an argument here. You’re trying to show them the greater dignity of the act: not superstition, but sacrifice. Not a mystery of matter, but a mystery of **will, love, and continuity**.

Jesus *is* the bread. But not in the bakery. In the body of the Church, torn and shared and resurrected every time we love one another as He did.
