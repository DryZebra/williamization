---

Your friend’s message is soaked in personal reverence, which makes it a great opportunity to pull back the curtain on some hidden assumptions. You don’t need to go nuclear—just start by politely questioning the frame they’ve imposed:

**1. “Tolkien is deeply Catholic; Lewis is Protestant.”**  
Sure, that’s technically true in biographical terms. But it doesn’t follow that the *works* themselves can be cleanly mapped onto denominational theology. Tolkien explicitly hated allegory, whereas Lewis embraced it. That distinction matters more than the Catholic-Protestant axis. Lewis wants Aslan to be Jesus. Tolkien doesn’t want Frodo to be Jesus—he wants Frodo to *suffer* like a Christian, and fail like a man.

**2. “Tolkien is far superior because fantasy is secondary.”**  
This is a strange claim unless you’re equating ‘fantasy’ with childishness. Lewis wrote fantasy for children; Tolkien wrote it for grown-ups—but neither makes fantasy secondary. In both, the fantastical *is* the theological. The talking animals, the resurrection of Gandalf, the lembas bread—they are not just sprinkles on a message; they are the message in motion. Your friend is confusing *tone* with *depth*. Just because Tolkien is darker and slower doesn’t mean it’s “more important.”

**3. “CS Lewis wrote a children’s book.”**  
This is true in the sense that Lewis intended *The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe* as a tale for children—but to stop there is to miss the real genius. Lewis uses the naivety of children’s literature to *smuggle theology*. If Tolkien is the cathedral, Lewis is the street preacher. And like all good preachers, he knows how to cast demons into pigs, if that’s what it takes to be understood.

**4. “Tolkien wrote a complete epic quest with real humanity.”**  
So did Homer. So did Lewis. So did Wu Cheng’en in *Journey to the West*. Tolkien does it through long landscapes and tight companionships; Lewis does it through sudden moral turns and reversals. Tolkien’s “humanity” comes from persistence. Lewis’s comes from repentance. Which one is better? That depends on your theology. If you’re Catholic, maybe it’s better to grind it out like Frodo. If you’re Protestant, maybe it’s better to collapse into grace like Edmund. But calling one “more human” ignores how both sides define what it means to be human.

**5. “The fantasy is secondary to the themes, worldbuilding, plot and characters.”**  
This is a very modern literary hierarchy: that *worldbuilding* and *themes* matter more than enchantment or wonder. But Lewis would flip that completely. He’d say fantasy *is* the theology—it’s what makes theology sensible to humans. Narnia is not a “place” so much as a *lens*. By contrast, Tolkien is building a world that could stand without the reader—it doesn't *need* you to believe it. That’s its Catholicism: the Church stands whether or not you kneel. Narnia disappears when the children stop believing. That’s Protestantism: faith is the key to vision.

**6. “There are sooo many parallels in the Lord of the Rings and Catholicism that make me love both more.”**  
Totally valid. But this is devotional, not analytical. There are also tons of Neopagan parallels, Norse mythology, and philological obsessions that make Tolkien more of a pre-Christian conservative than a post-Tridentine catechist. Tolkien’s “Catholicism” was ethnic, medieval, and tribal. Not Vatican II devotional. Your friend is reading Tolkien like a convert reads Chesterton: backward-facing, nostalgia-heavy, and romanticized.
