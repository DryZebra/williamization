> “It was given to her to clothe herself in fine linen, bright and clean; for the fine linen is the righteous acts of the saints.” (Rev 19:8) citeturn0search5

### 🛡️ Why "acts" instead of just "righteousness"?

1. **Textual Clarity & Symbolism**  
   The phrase explicitly ties the fine linen to “righteous acts.” Willmington, along with many commentators, sees this as deliberate: the bride’s purity is not theoretical but expressed through **lives lived in obedience and holiness**, making her truly prepared for the marriage supper. citeturn0search5

2. **Holistic View of Righteousness**  
   While imputed righteousness refers to Christ’s work credited to believers, **holy living validates** that profession. Thus, the “fine linen” symbolizes **active righteousness**—the works that flow from a genuine transformation.

3. **Consistent Biblical Imagery**  
   Many commentaries (e.g., Moody Bible Commentary) align with this approach, interpreting the white garments as **action-oriented evidence** of righteousness, especially in the context of preparation for a wedding banquet. citeturn0search5

- **Imputed righteousness** positions believers before God.
- **Righteous acts (fine linen)** are the evidence of that transformation—**what sanctified lives look like in practice**.
- Willmington’s interpretation is grounded in a biblical metaphor (the bride making herself ready) that underscores both identity and conduct.

## Assistant

Great question. The translation of *Revelation 19:8* as “the righteous **acts** of the saints” instead of simply “righteousness” often raises red flags—especially for those sensitive to *faith vs. works* debates. So why would someone like Dr. Willmington, from a *faith-alone* tradition, use this translation?

Let’s break it down:

---

### 📖 The Greek Word: *dikaiōmata*

The original Greek word in Revelation 19:8 is **δικαιώματα (dikaiōmata)**. This word **does not** simply mean *righteousness* (*dikaiosynē*, the general moral or legal state of being righteous).

Instead, *dikaiōmata* is more specific—it means **“righteous deeds,” “acts of justice,” or “ordinances fulfilled.”**

It’s the same root used in:
- Romans 2:26 – “the righteous **requirements** of the Law”
- Romans 5:18 – “one act of righteousness” (*dikaiōma*) brought justification

So, most modern translators render Revelation 19:8 this way to **stay true to the Greek**, not to introduce “works-based” theology.

---

### 🧠 Why this doesn’t contradict *faith alone* theology

Dr. Willmington and others in the conservative Baptist tradition **do not teach salvation by works**. But they affirm a critical distinction:

> **Works do not save you. But if you are saved, works will follow.**

Thus, the fine linen in Rev 19:8 (representing the Bride of Christ) symbolizes **the visible outcome of genuine faith**—not the basis of salvation, but the *evidence* of it.

---

### 🏗️ Why this still matters

You're asking: “Why phrase it that way and risk confusing the message?”

Because Revelation is **not about how you're saved**—it’s about **the culmination of the saved**. The bride is already redeemed. The linen doesn’t *make* her the bride. It’s her wedding garment, earned **after** being chosen.

So, the “righteous acts” emphasize the role of sanctification (living faithfully), **not justification** (being saved).

---

### 🎯 Bottom line

- They didn’t corrupt the message—they translated *dikaiōmata* literally.
- Willmington and others use “righteous acts” to teach that **true faith transforms people**, and that transformation *shows*.
- It fits Baptist theology: **faith saves**, **acts prove**.
