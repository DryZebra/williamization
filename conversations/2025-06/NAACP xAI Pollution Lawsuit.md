---

### 🏭 **Why workers live near factories in China**
- **Urban villages**: Many factories are built near or inside dense "urban villages" (*城中村*, *chengzhongcun*), especially in export-oriented provinces like Guangdong. Workers rent cheap rooms next to or inside industrial parks.
- **Factory towns**: In places like **Shenzhen**, **Dongguan**, or **Zhengzhou** (a.k.a. "iPhone City"), entire cities are shaped around a single industrial function—clothing, electronics, buttons, shoes, toys, etc.
- **Migrant labor model**: Millions of rural Chinese leave their homes and live temporarily in factory dorms. The state relies on this **internal migration** as a cheap, flexible labor force.

---

### 🚧 **How China deals with pollution near residential zones**

#### 1. **Zoning + Compartmentalization**
China creates **Special Economic Zones (SEZs)** and **development zones** where industrial and residential use are tightly managed—even if close together.
- These zones are **planned from the top down**, allowing co-location of work and living spaces with some enforced separation of hazards.

#### 2. **Environmental Sacrifice Zones**
In many cases, the Chinese government has simply **accepted pollution** in certain areas as the cost of development:
- Rural or poor urban outskirts are often chosen for high-pollution industry.
- Locals, especially migrants, are considered disposable in practice, if not on paper.
- In major cities, visible pollution may be cleaned up or relocated to hinterlands, exporting the damage.

#### 3. **“Green Upgrades” under Pressure**
After mass protests and international pressure, China:
- Began **moving toxic industry** away from dense residential zones in wealthy cities.
- Enforced **emission standards** on paper, especially for export-facing factories.
- Invested in **clean tech** or **vertical integration** (e.g. Foxconn trying to build greener facilities).

Still, **enforcement is uneven**: some factories install scrubbers only to turn them off at night.

#### 4. **State Control of Movement + Protest**
- Since public protest is tightly restricted, **complaints rarely lead to change** unless they threaten broader stability.
- Workers who fall sick often have no legal path to fight back. Healthcare and legal recourse are minimal for migrants.

---

### 🧠 What does this mean?
China didn’t “solve” the contradiction of **industrial scale + human proximity**—it **optimized around it**:
- It treated labor and living conditions as variables that can be controlled.
- It prioritized **economic output** and **social harmony** over individual health or long-term ecological sustainability.
- It now tries to “green” these zones **without disrupting production**—but only when public outrage, diplomatic pressure, or economic conditions demand it.
