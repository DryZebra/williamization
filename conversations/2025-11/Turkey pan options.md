# Turkey pan options

What can I use as a pan for my turkey? How much juice comes out?

## Assistant

Here’s the real, practical answer:

### **What you can use as a turkey pan**
You can use **anything that is:**
✅ Oven-safe up to **400°F+**  
✅ At least **2–3 inches deep**  
✅ Big enough that the turkey fits without touching the sides

Good options if you don’t have a roasting pan:
- A **rimmed baking sheet + wire rack** (best DIY option)
- A **large casserole dish**
- A **clean disposable aluminum pan** (cheap and works great)
- A **deep cast iron pan** (if the turkey fits)
- Even a **clean, heavy cookie sheet with raised edges** in a pinch

**DO NOT use:** glass if it’s thin or cracked, anything non-oven-safe, or flimsy foil alone (it can collapse).

---

### **How much juice comes out?**
A LOT more than you think.

For a typical turkey:
- A **12–15 lb turkey** can release **2–4 cups of liquid**
- Bigger birds can release **up to 6 cups**

This comes from:
- Water content in the meat
- Melted fat
- Any injected solution or brine

That’s why your pan **must be deep** — shallow pans will overflow and make a greasy mess in your oven.

---

### **Pro tip so you don’t drown the turkey**
If you want **roast texture instead of boiled texture**:
- Put the turkey on a **rack or veggies (onions, carrots, celery)** so it sits ABOVE the liquid.
- Liquid should **never cover the bird** while roasting.

## Assistant

Short answer: you **don’t want the turkey sitting in its own juice** if you’re aiming for a classic roasted bird with crispy skin.

Here’s how to keep it up out of the liquid:

### Best Ways to Elevate the Turkey

**1. Use a rack (ideal)**
- A metal roasting rack inside your pan keeps the turkey lifted while juices drip down.
- This gives you crispy skin and better airflow.

**2. No rack? Use vegetables**
Make a cheap “natural rack” with:
- Thick slices of **onion**
- Big chunks of **carrots**
- Celery stalks

Lay them in the pan in a pile and set the turkey on top. This:
- Lifts the bird
- Flavors the drippings for gravy
- Prevents sogginess

**3. Crumpled foil rack**
If you’ve got nothing else:
- Roll thick foil into **thick logs**
- Form a circle or crosshatch in the pan
- Place turkey on top

This works surprisingly well.

---

### When would you *want* it in the juices?
Only if you’re doing:
- A **covered braise-style turkey**
- Or cooking for **shredded meat**, not crisp skin

That’s a totally different texture.

---

### Rule of thumb
If you want:
✅ Crispy brown skin → **keep it elevated**  
✅ Super soft, fall-apart meat → **let it sit in liquid**

## Assistant

Short answer: **do not use copper wire** on your turkey.

Here’s why, straight and practical:

### Why copper is a bad idea
When copper gets hot with:
- Salt  
- Turkey juices  
- Heat for hours  

…it can:
- React with acids and salts
- Leach metallic flavors into the meat
- Potentially create copper residue in the drippings

Also, #10 solid copper gets **very hot** and stays hot, which can:
- Burn the skin where it touches
- Cause uneven cooking

---

### What you *should* use instead

If you don’t have cooking twine, your safe options are:

✅ **100% cotton butcher’s twine** (best)  
✅ **Dental floss** *only if it is plain, unflavored, no wax, no mint*  
✅ A strip of **clean, untreated cotton cloth** (like from an old clean t-shirt)

---

### If you have NOTHING safe
You don’t actually have to tie the legs.

You can just:
- Tuck the wings under the body
- Let the legs rest naturally

It’ll still cook fine and get crispy.

---

### Bottom line
Copper wire = **no**  
Food-safe twine or cloth = **yes**  
No tying at all = totally fine ✅

## User

How do I know if the turkey is done with no thermometer?

## Assistant



## Assistant

Totally fair question — you can do this **without a thermometer** if you know what to look for.

Here’s how to tell your turkey is done:

---

## 1. Check the leg joint (most reliable)

Grab the **drumstick** and wiggle it.

✅ If it moves **easily and feels loose**, it’s done  
❌ If it feels tight or stiff, it needs more time

You should be able to move the leg without tearing the skin.

---

## 2. Look at the juices

Take a knife and poke the **thickest part of the breast** or between thigh and body.

✅ Clear juices = done  
❌ Pink/red juices = needs more time

If it looks bloody or cloudy, give it more oven time.

---

## 3. Skin color test

✅ Deep golden brown = good  
❌ Pale or rubbery looking = not done

If it looks right but you’re unsure, tent it loosely with foil and keep cooking.

---

## 4. Cooking time fallback rule

If you want a simple safety guideline:

**Cook uncovered at 350°F for ~15 minutes per pound**

Examples:
- 12 lb → ~3 hours
- 14 lb → ~3.5 hours
- 18 lb → ~4.5 hours

Start checking for doneness about **30 mins before** the estimated time.

---

## 5. Absolute emergency check

If you slice into the breast:
- Meat should be **white, not translucent**
- Texture should be **firm but juicy**, not rubbery

---

### Important:
Let it rest **30 minutes MINIMUM** before cutting so juices redistribute.
