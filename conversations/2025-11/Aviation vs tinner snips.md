# Aviation vs tinner snips

What's the difference between aviation and tinner snips?

## Assistant

Here’s the real-world difference in plain terms:

**They’re the same tool — just different names.**

- **“Aviation snips”** is the formal/trade name (because they were originally developed for cutting aircraft sheet metal).
- **“Tinner snips”** is the older trade name (used by tin/metal workers — “tinners”).

Today, when you see:
- Red handle
- Green handle
- Yellow handle snips

…those are **aviation snips**, but a lot of old-school guys still call them **tinner snips**.

### What people *sometimes* mean (confusion source):

Some folks use **“tinner snips”** to mean the **straight-blade, older style** tin snips — like this:

✅ Long straight handles  
✅ No compound leverage  
✅ More like scissors

While **aviation snips** usually mean:
✅ Short, sturdy handles  
✅ Compound leverage (mechanical advantage)  
✅ Color-coded for left/right/straight cuts

### Practical takeaway:

If someone says:
- **“Grab the tinners”** or  
- **“Where are the aviation snips?”**

They mean the same family of tools unless they specifically say:
- “Straight tin snips” vs  
- “Left/right aviation snips”

If you tell me what you’re cutting (duct, studs, MC armor, flashing, etc.), I can tell you which one actually works best.

## Assistant

Good photos — this makes it easy to explain.

**They aren’t different because of brands.**  
They’re shaped different because they’re built for **different types of metal work and different cutting behavior.**

Here’s what you’re actually seeing:

---

## 1. Your Milwaukee pair (first photo) = **Aviation / Compound Action Snips**

These are:
- Short, thick jaws
- Curved + pointed tip
- Multiple pivot points (compound leverage)

**Why they look that way:**
That curved, bulky shape gives:
- More cutting force
- Control when following curves
- Less hand fatigue
- Ability to cut heavier gauge sheet metal

These are meant for:
✅ Ductwork  
✅ Sheet metal  
✅ HVAC  
✅ Flashing  
✅ Thinner steel / aluminum  

That shape **is the classic aviation snip design**.

---

## 2. Crescent ones (second photo) = **Traditional “Tinner” / Straight Tin Snips**

These are:
- Longer, straighter blades
- Bigger scissor-style shape
- Single pivot (no compound leverage)

**Why they look different:**
These are designed for:
- Straight cuts
- Light gauge metal
- Faster, longer cuts
- Less mechanical force

Old-school tin knockers, roofers, and duct guys used these before aviation snips became standard.

---

## The real difference in use

| Feature | Aviation Snips (Milwaukee) | Tin/Tinner Snips (Crescent) |
|--------|-----------------------------|-----------------------------|
| Handle style | Short, fat, compound | Long, scissor style |
| Power | High leverage | Low leverage |
| Control | Better for curves | Better for long straight cuts |
| Hand fatigue | Lower | Higher |
| Metal thickness | Handles heavier | Best for lighter metal |

---

## Why stores carry both

Because:
- HVAC guys want **aviation snips**
- Roofers or classic sheet metal guys sometimes want **traditional tinners**
- Electricians mostly use aviation snips

---

## Trade secret / electrician context

For guys like us:

✅ Aviation snips are better 90% of the time  
✅ Milwaukee, Wiss, Midwest are the good ones  
⚠️ Cheap long-blade tinners will twist metal on duct or stud edging

---

### Bottom line

They aren’t brand-shaped different.

They’re **design families**:
- **Milwaukee = Modern aviation snips**
- **Crescent = Traditional tin/tinner snips**
