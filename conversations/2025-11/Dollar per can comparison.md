### Key mechanisms for the difference  
- **Volume discounts**: Bulk orders reduce per-unit packaging, shipping, handling costs.  
- **Larger can sizes / less premium branding**: Bulk often uses larger sizes (e.g., #10 cans) or less premium brands, which reduces cost per serving.  
- **Lower retail margin**: Retail stores carry overhead (shelf space, smaller order sizes, marketing) that adds cost to the consumer.  
- **Food-pantry networks / charitable organizations may receive donations or discounted rates**, further reducing cost to the pantry.  
- **Less packaging / fewer intermediary markups**: Bulk food services often deal directly with distributors or manufacturers and skip some middle-retail markups.

---

### Rough “how far a dollar goes” estimate  
- If a consumer pays ~$1.50 for a 15 oz can, then $1 buys ~0.67 of a can (i.e., you’d need ~$1.50 for one full can).  
- In a bulk/institution setting, if you can buy a can (or equivalent size) for ~$0.90 (just illustrative), then $1 buys ~1.11 cans.  
- If further discounting (e.g., very large case size or donation) brings cost to ~$0.50 per can, then $1 buys ~2 cans.

So, as a **rule of thumb**, for many food-pantry/bulk environments you might expect **roughly 1.5 to 3 times as many cans per dollar** compared to regular consumer retail.

### Caveats & things to check  
- The size or weight of the canned unit matters (15 oz vs 29 oz vs #10 can).  
- The contents matter (beans/vegetables vs meat/fish or specialty).  
- Shipping, storage, handling, spoilage, and pantry administrative cost still exist (so “free food” to a client still has cost behind the scenes).
