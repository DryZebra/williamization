### 1.  The Function of a Grand Narrative
A grand narrative isn’t just a plot; it’s **the myth that lets a worldview feel inevitable**.  
It gives ordinary readers a way to *live* the philosophy before they can *explain* it.

It must therefore:
1. **Embody** the religion’s moral logic through motion, not sermons.  
2. **Give readers emotional rehearsal** for faith, loss, duty, and renewal.  
3. **End with continuity**, not final peace—so that readers carry the motion forward.

---

### 2.  What the Goal Was in *The Lord of the Rings*

| Layer | Tolkien’s Goal |
|-------|----------------|
| **Religious** | Show Catholic grace without naming it. Evil cannot create; good can only *pity* and *endure*. Salvation comes through humility. |
| **Moral** | Every being is tested by *power*. Refusing domination is holiness. |
| **Cultural** | Give England a native epic myth — a pre-Christian moral soil that points toward Christianity. |
| **Emotional Arc** | Innocence → Temptation → Sacrifice → Fading Grace. “Eucatastrophe”: a sudden redemption that proves providence. |

So LotR’s *goal* is not “destroy the Ring” but *prove that mercy redeems even the damned (Gollum)* and that history bends toward grace through small, faithful acts.

---

### 3.  What the Goal Was in *The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe*

| Layer | Lewis’s Goal |
|-------|---------------|
| **Religious** | Teach Protestant salvation: substitutionary atonement and resurrection through Aslan’s death. |
| **Moral** | Childlike faith > adult cynicism. Love and obedience overcome rational evil. |
| **Cultural** | Translate Christian myth for children; re-enchant post-war England with moral clarity. |
| **Emotional Arc** | Betrayal → Sacrifice → Resurrection → Kingship → Return to innocence. |

Its *goal* is not defeating the Witch, but showing that *love willingly dies to forgive betrayal*.

---

### 4.  Therefore…

**Tolkien** = Christianity seen from *below* — history, endurance, pity.  
**Lewis** = Christianity seen from *above* — atonement, forgiveness, authority.

Both transform doctrine into *felt inevitability*: “Of course the world works like this.”

> **To make motion sacred.**

Where Tolkien sought grace, and Lewis sought redemption,  
**you seek continuity** — the revelation that *God is the process that never stops moving toward understanding.*
