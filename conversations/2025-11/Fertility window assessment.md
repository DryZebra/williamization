## 1. How the fertile window usually works

Key facts:

- Ovulation happens about **14 days *before* the next period**, not a fixed number of days after the last one.

  - plus **ovulation day itself**

So for a textbook 28-day cycle:
- Fertile window ≈ **cycle days 9–14**.
- Sex in that range can absolutely lead to pregnancy.

That’s a really good question — and it’s one of those topics that’s surrounded by tons of old advice, but biologically speaking, **there’s no reliable natural method** to choose your child’s sex. Here’s how it actually works:  

---

### ⚗️ The biological reality
- Each sperm cell carries **either an X or a Y chromosome**.  
- The egg always carries an **X**.  
- XX → girl; XY → boy.  
- The ratio of X- to Y-bearing sperm is **almost perfectly 1:1**, and although some tiny variations exist, the difference isn’t consistent or controllable.

So yes — statistically it’s *roughly* 50/50 every time, though global data shows a **slight** bias toward boys (about 105 males per 100 females born) due to small biological and environmental effects, not anything you can reliably tweak.

---

### ⚛️ What people try — and why it doesn’t hold up
You may have heard of things like:
- **Timing** (the “Shettles Method”): idea that Y sperm are faster but die sooner, X sperm are slower but hardier.  
- **pH changes / diet tweaks:** claims that vaginal acidity or mineral intake influences which sperm survive.  
- **Positions or depth of penetration:** said to favor one or the other based on where sperm are deposited.

Scientific reviews over decades have shown that **none of these have repeatable, significant effects**. They might shift odds by a few percentage points at most, and even that’s uncertain.

---

### 🧬 The only proven methods
The only techniques with real control are medical:
1. **Sperm sorting + IUI/IVF (e.g., MicroSort)** – separates X and Y sperm in a lab; accuracy around 70–90%.  
2. **Preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) with IVF** – embryos are tested before transfer; accuracy ~99%, but it’s invasive, expensive, and usually reserved for medical or ethical reasons (e.g., avoiding sex-linked diseases).
