### 🇺🇸 **The American art world in 1988**
By 1988, the U.S. art scene had split into two main camps:

1. **Neo-Expressionism fading out**  
   The early ’80s wave (Basquiat, Schnabel, Salle, Clemente) had peaked around 1983–1986. By 1988, that fire was dying, partly because Basquiat had just died, and the market was saturated. Collectors were moving to “conceptual coolness.”  
   - *Result*: Fewer mythic, emotional paintings. More ironic, textual, detached.

2. **The Conceptual / Post-Modern turn**  
   Artists like Barbara Kruger, Jeff Koons, Cindy Sherman, Sherrie Levine, and Christopher Wool were the stars. Their art deliberately rejected grand narratives and spiritual purpose. It was about *signs*, *advertising*, *media*, and *identity*.  
   - Koons’s **“Banality”** series (1988) — porcelain sculptures like *Michael Jackson and Bubbles* — became the icon of this shift: kitsch as philosophy.  
   - Kruger’s **“Your Body Is a Battleground”** (1989, made just after 1988) perfectly captures the era’s tone — political, ironic, declarative, mass-media style.

So, while the Soviet Union was painting apocalyptic realism and sacred nationhood, America was obsessed with *irony, media, and consumerism*.

---

### ⚖️ **Why the contrast**
- **Material context:**  
  The USSR was on the edge of collapse; artists felt history’s weight. In the U.S., the Cold War seemed “won,” the economy was booming, and moral seriousness was out of fashion.  
- **Market logic:**  
  American art had become investment. Dealers like Larry Gagosian and Leo Castelli were minting millionaires, not prophets.  
- **Philosophical mood:**  
  Post-modernism explicitly rejected “grand narratives.” To make something like *The Eternal Russia* in 1988 America would have been considered *reactionary* or “naïve.”
