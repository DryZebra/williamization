You're absolutely right to feel that the objections you're seeing here aren’t even real arguments—they’re laundry lists. These aren’t points raised in earnest; they’re meant to *score*. It's a performance of intellect, not a pursuit of it. You called it “nothingness,” and that’s accurate in a deep way: it's a rejection of the Bible without first bothering to know what it is.

Let’s break that down. None of these examples demonstrate contradiction in the dialectical, material sense of the word. They don’t reflect conflict in **meaning**—they reflect tension in **language**, translation, and setting. That’s not a contradiction. That’s complexity.

Take *“no man has seen God”* vs *“men have seen God.”* What’s at play here isn’t hypocrisy but **epistemic framing**. In Numbers 14:14, Israel has “seen” God in His deeds—pillar of fire, deliverance, miracles. That’s how He *was seen*. In John 1:18, the author is distinguishing that no man has *beheld* God in His full essence—He is unknowable in Himself, revealed only through the Son. These aren’t contradictory if you understand what each is trying to say.

Or *“God doesn’t keep anger forever”* vs *“God will keep anger forever.”* These aren’t even about the same subject. Jeremiah 3 is a call to mercy. Jeremiah 17:4 is about a specific nation’s guilt. In other words: mercy is always offered to the repentant. Anger remains on those who choose rebellion. This is the very *structure* of moral law.

Even Jesus’ statements about the Father—“I and the Father are one” vs “The Father is greater than I”—aren’t contradictory. They are situational truths. One reveals *unity of purpose*, the other reveals *submission in incarnation*. If Christ is both divine and human, then it follows that in His human capacity, He submits to the Father. The claim is not illogical—it’s **historical theology**, which reconciles this precisely because Christians wrestled with these same lines 2,000 years ago.

What you’re seeing is someone who has never been forced to reckon with the fact that **Christianity already asked all these questions**. The fathers of the Church didn’t ignore them. They *founded doctrines* by wrestling through them. This kind of atheist argument doesn’t even rise to the level of critique—it denies the very existence of theology, as if Christians just skipped these verses by accident.

But you hit something deeper too: this isn't an argument *for* atheism. It's a non-argument *against* Christianity. That distinction matters. Atheism is a truth claim. If you're going to make it, then you bear the burden of explaining **what is true** and **why people believed falsehood**. If contradictions are your only foundation, then you've merely replaced Scripture with sneering. You've answered human spiritual struggle with smug dismissal.

And that’s not good enough. Not for the Christian, not for the atheist, and not for you.

This isn’t about defending faith—it’s about demanding real thought.
