## Assistant

Jesus’ teaching about “cutting off your hand” appears in several places, including:

- **Matthew 5:29–30**
- **Mark 9:43–48**

These statements are hyperbolic commands to tear out one's eye or cut off one’s hand if they cause sin. Here’s the context for each:

---

### **1. Matthew 5:29–30 — Sermon on the Mount**
> _"If your right eye causes you to sin, tear it out and throw it away... And if your right hand causes you to sin, cut it off and throw it away..."_

**Audience:** A crowd of ordinary Jews, not elites. Jesus is addressing the masses gathered to hear Him on the mountain (Matthew 5:1), including His disciples.

**Context:** This is part of the *Sermon on the Mount*, specifically in a section (Matthew 5:17–48) where Jesus is escalating the Law—not abolishing it, but *fulfilling* it by revealing its moral depths. He begins with “You have heard it said…” (e.g., about murder, adultery), then raises the stakes.

Jesus is not talking to Pharisees or Sadducees directly here—He’s correcting popular understandings of righteousness. He’s telling *normal people* that sin starts in the heart. Cutting off the hand is about removing the cause of sin entirely. It’s a metaphor for **radical repentance**. The Law forbids adultery, but Christ forbids the lust that leads to it. The Law forbids murder, but Christ forbids the hate that breeds it.

**Moral Point:** Perfection isn’t legalistic rule-following. It’s a total war against corruption. Anything that keeps you from righteousness must be destroyed.

---

### **2. Mark 9:43–48 — After the Transfiguration**
> _"If your hand causes you to sin, cut it off… better for you to enter life maimed than with two hands to go to hell."_

**Audience:** Jesus is speaking to His disciples privately (Mark 9:30–37), just after they’ve been arguing about who is the greatest.

**Context:** This is a warning after a rebuke. The disciples had argued about hierarchy—status in the Kingdom. Jesus responds with childlike humility as the requirement. Then He warns them of judgment if they cause others to stumble or tolerate sin in themselves. The “cut off your hand” passage is part of a **warning to teachers and leaders** that accountability is eternal.

**Moral Point:** Power doesn’t excuse sin. In fact, it magnifies responsibility. Leading others astray, or refusing to crucify your own sinful instincts, can damn you. Jesus is not being symbolic for drama—He’s laying out a strict moral reality.

---

### **So—Pharisees or Sadducees?**
Neither, directly. In both contexts, Jesus is speaking to:
- **Common people** (Matthew 5) who assumed righteousness was legal externalism.
- **His own disciples** (Mark 9), warning them against complacency, pride, and hypocrisy.

But these teachings *do* stand in contrast to the Pharisees’ attitude: outward lawfulness masking inward decay. Jesus is revealing that the true fulfillment of the Law is not better performance—it is transformation, at any cost.

## Assistant

Yes, I remember—and that concern is legitimate. A lot of Jesus’ hardest sayings *can* be read as traps set for the Pharisees: “Go ahead. Be perfect. Cut off your hand. Fulfill the law without hypocrisy—if you can.” But this particular saying, while it *does* resonate with that challenge, isn’t aimed at the Pharisees alone.

Let’s look at the evidence.

---

### **Matthew 5 (Sermon on the Mount):**
- Jesus is explicitly teaching His disciples and the gathered crowd—**not** debating Pharisees.
- He opens this section (v17–20) by saying:
  > “Unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven.”

This is not an affirmation of Pharisaic law-keeping. It’s a **condemnation** of it. He’s saying: *You think they’re righteous? They’re not. You’ll need something much deeper.* What follows is a set of moral escalations that prove **nobody** can fulfill the law with their body alone. Heart, mind, and intent matter too.

> “You have heard it said… but I say to you…”

By the time He says to “cut off your hand,” He’s already *refuted* the idea that external lawfulness is enough. So while this line might sound like, “Fine then—cut off your hand,” that’s not His tone here. He is not arguing. He is **teaching**.

So this is not a sarcastic trap for the Pharisees. It’s a *dead-serious* moral claim. The Kingdom is coming—and to enter it, you must be willing to destroy everything that corrupts you. That’s not law-perfection. That’s moral rebirth.

---

### **Mark 9 (Disciples only):**
In this version, the “cut off your hand” passage follows:
- A private conversation after Jesus catches them **arguing about greatness**.
- A warning that it’s better to have a millstone around your neck than to cause **a child** to stumble.
- An instruction to pluck out anything that causes sin, or risk being cast into **Gehenna**.

There are no Pharisees here. Just *those closest to Him*. And He’s telling them: *You are not immune. You must fear sin, even in yourself.*

So again: **not a taunt** to legalists. It’s a terrifying, real warning to His own people.

---

### **So what’s the material truth?**
This teaching *includes* the Pharisees—but transcends them.

- Yes, it *exposes* the futility of law-based perfection: no amount of amputation will purify a corrupt heart.
- But it’s **also** the first glimpse of what Jesus will later demand outright: *Die to yourself. Be born again. Leave everything behind.*
- This is a *moral ultimatum*, not a theological loophole. He is saying: **If sin owns you, kill it before it kills you.**

That includes us, Gentiles and all. Not as law-followers. As humans.
