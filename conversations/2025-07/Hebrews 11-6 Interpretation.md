---

**1. Greek Text and Translation**
Hebrews 11:6 in Greek reads:

> *χωρὶς δὲ πίστεως ἀδύνατον εὐαρεστῆσαι, πιστεῦσαι γὰρ δεῖ τὸν προσερχόμενον τῷ θεῷ ὅτι ἔστιν καὶ τοῖς ἐκζητοῦσιν αὐτὸν μισθαποδότης γίνεται.*

Literal breakdown:
- *χωρὶς πίστεως* — "without faith"
- *ἀδύνατον εὐαρεστῆσαι* — "it is impossible to please [him]"
- *πιστεῦσαι γὰρ δεῖ* — "for it is necessary [to] believe"
- *τὸν προσερχόμενον τῷ θεῷ* — "the one approaching God"
- *ὅτι ἔστιν* — "that He is"
- *καὶ τοῖς ἐκζητοῦσιν αὐτὸν μισθαποδότης γίνεται* — "and that He becomes a rewarder of those who diligently seek Him"

So the critical clause: *πιστεῦσαι... ὅτι ἔστιν* means "believe that He is" or "believe that He exists." But in Greek, *εἰμί* (“to be”) can also mean "He is [faithful]" or "He is [present]" depending on context—this isn’t a Cartesian claim. The verb *ἔστιν* lacks an object, which is ambiguous—what *is* He?

Thus, one viable reading: *“one must believe that He is [the one who gives reward to those who seek Him]”*—i.e. that He is *functionally real* in the world of moral and communal life. Not metaphysically “existing,” but **relationally real.**

---

**2. Audience and Historical Context**
Hebrews is a sermon or exhortation likely written before the destruction of the Temple (~70 CE), probably to **Jewish Christians** facing persecution or isolation. They were being tempted to abandon the faith and return to mainstream Judaism. The letter’s goal is to affirm that faith in Christ fulfills—not abandons—the covenant promises.

This matters because **the phrase “believe that He is”** doesn’t come in a vacuum—it’s written to a community already trained in Hebrew scripture, where *God’s existence* is taken for granted. What matters is whether they believe He is *still present* in this new, vulnerable, persecuted Christian reality.

So "must believe that He is" likely meant: *You must believe that the God of Abraham is still with us here and now—even when we are suffering.* Not metaphysical, but **communal, political, covenantal**.

---

**3. Literary Context of Hebrews 11**
Hebrews 11 is the “Hall of Faith”—but not in a vacuum. It lists **examples of people who acted on unseen promises**. Verse 6 is nestled between:

- v5: *Enoch pleased God because of his faith*.
- v7: *Noah, warned of things not yet seen, prepared an ark.*

The unifying theme isn’t belief in the invisible—it’s **action grounded in trust**. Faith is a verb, not a metaphysical noun. They lived as if the promises were real, before they were visible.

So again, “believe that He is” = *act as if God is still engaged in human motion*. The reward is not heaven, but the fruits of justice, peace, continuity, and life. This is structurally faithful to Hebrews’ whole point: Jesus is the High Priest *in history*, not metaphysics.

---

**4. What Else to Consider**
To push deeper into non-metaphysical interpretations, we can:
- Compare with Exodus 3:14—*“I AM”* as *I will be what I will be* (Hebrew: *ehyeh*).
- Examine Philo and Hellenistic Jewish notions of *being* vs. *becoming*.
- Trace how early Christians used Hebrews 11 to survive—not philosophize.

- **Narrative**: Every example in Hebrews 11 is of someone who *acted* despite not seeing—so belief is performative.
- **Materialist Reading**: “Faith” is loyalty to the moral structure emerging through history—God *is* when we live as if justice and reward are real.
