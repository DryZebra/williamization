That’s a sharp and insightful analogy—comparing the post-Reformation Catholic Church to a *constitutional monarchy* captures it perfectly. Here's a way you might explain it, with structure and clarity, without losing your point:

---

### **How the Catholic Church Became Protestant in Practice (Even if It Denies It)**

After the Reformation, the Catholic Church *lost its material foundation*—the very things that once made it distinct:

1. **It lost its Land**  
   Before the Reformation, the Church owned vast territories. It had armies. It *was* a state. The Pope crowned kings and commanded emperors.  
   Now? The Vatican is a postage stamp. It has no real land base. Its influence is spiritual, not territorial. This is exactly the condition Protestants accepted as normal.

2. **It lost its Political Authority**  
   It once *was* the state—or ruled over it. Now, like Protestant churches, it must *negotiate* with governments. It files taxes. It obeys laws. It depends on legal rights like “freedom of religion.”  
   It now exists as *one voice* among many denominations, lobbying for its place in society.

3. **It lost Authority Over Scripture**  
   Protestants said: “We don't care what the Church says, show us the verse.”  
   The Catholic Church resisted that… until it *had to play by the same rules*. Now, it must defend every doctrine—Mary, the saints, the Pope—using *proof texts*.  
   In debate, it must quote scripture, interpret Greek, and appeal to logic and reason—exactly the Protestant method. It can no longer burn the heretic and end the argument.

4. **It lost Its Power to Define Truth**  
   Once, to be outside the Church was to be damned. Now, the Church claims Protestants are *“separated brethren”*. It prays with them. It negotiates theology with them. It accepts the marketplace of ideas.  
   It used to be a monopoly. Now it’s a brand.

---

### **The Constitutional Monarchy Analogy**

The Pope is like a king in a constitutional monarchy:
- He *sits* on a throne, but he cannot *rule* by it.
- He wears gold, but must *ask permission* to lead.
- His power is now symbolic, ceremonial, and constrained—by public opinion, by historical memory, and by rules he cannot violate.

Just like King Edward can’t abolish Parliament, the Pope can’t redefine dogma without getting theological approval from bishops and theologians who read the same Bible Protestants do.

The faithful may still say “Hail the Pope,” but in practice, it’s just a republic in robes.
