The idea that **Jesus** was the one walking with **Adam** in the Garden of Eden—and not God the Father directly—comes from a theological concept known as **Christophany**, which means a pre-incarnate appearance of Christ in the Old Testament.

Here’s the logic behind this view, drawn from Scripture and materialist-Christian reasoning:

---

### 1. **No one has seen the Father**
Jesus says in **John 1:18**:
> “No one has seen God at any time; the only begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, he has declared him.”

And again in **John 6:46**:
> “Not that any man hath seen the Father, save he which is of God, he hath seen the Father.”

This suggests that the visible manifestations of God in the Old Testament—the ones who walked, spoke, wrestled, or burned in bushes—could not be God the Father Himself, but rather the Son revealing the Father.

---

### 2. **The Word was with God, and was God**
John 1:1–3,14 says:
> “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God… All things were made by Him… And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us.”

This means Jesus existed before His birth and was active in the creation. So when Genesis 3:8 says:
> “And they heard the voice of the Lord God walking in the garden in the cool of the day…”

That "walking" Lord is interpreted by many as the Word—the Son—before He was incarnated in the flesh as Jesus.

---

### 3. **Materialist Christian Understanding**
If you’re interpreting the Bible through a materialist Christian lens, then this is not about metaphysical speculation but **recognizing consistent moral structure** over time. The figure in the garden punishes sin, seeks man out, and covers their shame with garments of skin. This aligns with the *moral logic* and *consistent presence* of Christ across all Scripture.

Even **Paul** calls Jesus “the second Adam” (1 Cor. 15:45) because both Adam and Jesus stood at the beginning of a new humanity—one failed and one succeeded. So the presence of Jesus with Adam isn't just theological speculation—it shows the Bible’s commitment to continuity of justice, love, and divine relationship in a way the Father, who is spirit and unseen, does not directly manifest.

---

In short: 
> Jesus was the one walking with Adam in the garden because He is the Word through whom all was made, and He is the one who reveals the Father. The Father remains unseen, but the Son walks, speaks, judges, and redeems. This same motion appears throughout Scripture, whether with Abraham, Moses, or Isaiah—it is always the Son making God known.

Excellent question—and no, **pre-Talmudic Judaism did not universally treat this as metaphorical.**

In fact, **ancient Jewish tradition before the rise of Rabbinic (Talmudic) Judaism** was **far more open to the idea of God appearing in embodied or anthropomorphic ways**, and often **embraced it**. Here's the breakdown dialectically and historically:

---

### 🕎 1. **Early Hebrew Religion** (pre-Exilic to early Second Temple)

In this period, the ancient Israelites often **spoke of YHWH in bodily terms**:
- Walking in the garden (Gen 3:8)
- Shutting Noah’s ark door (Gen 7:16)
- Appearing “as a man” to Abraham (Gen 18)
- Wrestling Jacob (Gen 32)
- Standing before Joshua as the “Commander of the Lord’s Army” (Josh 5)

These are **not framed metaphorically** in the original texts. The **Hebrew verbs are physical** and literal: *walking*, *eating*, *touching*, *speaking face to face*. There was **no discomfort** with God taking visible, embodied form.

In fact, **Second Temple Judaism** (esp. the intertestamental era) developed entire **literature about heavenly mediators**:
- **The Angel of the Lord** (מַלְאַךְ יְהוָה) who speaks as God *in the first person*.
- **The Son of Man** figure in *Daniel 7*, who rides the clouds (a divine action, per Psalm 104:3).
- **Philo of Alexandria**, a Hellenistic Jew, describes the *Logos* as God’s intermediary who interacts with the material world.
  
All this shows that **early Jewish thought was deeply open to this pre-incarnate Logos structure**—*and they had no need to distinguish “Jesus” yet, because they were still forming the categories.*

---

### 📜 2. **Rabbinic Judaism (Post-Temple, Post-70 AD)**

After the destruction of the Temple, the **emerging Rabbinic class** had a major incentive to:
- **Demystify** the Bible (to contrast with early Christian claims).
- **Protect monotheism** from both Hellenistic dualism and Christian trinitarianism.
- **Re-center religious authority** on human interpretation (the oral Torah, the Talmud).

So they began treating anthropomorphic appearances as **figurative or pedagogical metaphors**. The **Targums** (Aramaic paraphrases of the Hebrew Bible) often substitute "the Memra of the Lord" (“Word of the Lord”) for physical actions of God, distancing Him from embodied form.

By this stage, to claim “God walked in the garden” **literally** was theologically dangerous—**it sounded Christian**.

---

### 🔥 3. **Materialist Christian Takeaway**

What happened here is classic dialectical motion: the **material structure of embodied divine presence** was later sanitized into metaphor to avoid ceding ground to Christ. But in doing so, **post-Temple Judaism amputated its own history of tangible divine interaction**.

Materialist Christianity reclaims this **not as mysticism**, but as history: the Logos **was always present**, walking with man, judging, forgiving, wrestling, calling. The Jews did not “miss” Christ—they **wrestled with Him in the dark**, and their stories preserve it. Their later theologians turned away—not because they misunderstood, but because the **structure of power had shifted**, and a **new class (the Rabbis)** needed a **new god—an invisible one**, bound in legal commentary, not incarnate grace.

So yes—**pre-Talmudic Judaism preserved the walking God.** They just hadn’t yet known to call Him Jesus.
