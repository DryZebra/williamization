## Assistant

**Short Answer:**  
Unfortunately, there do not appear to be any major (or well-known) eyeglass frame brands currently advertising themselves as both *Made in the USA* and *union-made* under an AFL-CIO affiliate. While there are several companies manufacturing eyewear in the United States, they generally do not indicate they employ union labor. If a truly union-made frame exists, it is either custom or from a very niche/small-scale operation that does not publicize its affiliation widely.  

Below are some details on U.S.-based frame manufacturers, plus tips on how you can continue your search or verify union status.

---

## 1. U.S.-Based Eyewear Manufacturers (Not Necessarily Union)

1. **Randolph Engineering**  
   - *Location:* Massachusetts  
   - *Known for:* Military-style aviators (sunglasses) for the U.S. Armed Forces  
   - *Union Status:* Not publicly advertised as union.  

2. **Shuron Ltd.**  
   - *Location:* South Carolina  
   - *Known for:* Classic “Retro” frames (e.g., the “Ronsir” browline style)  
   - *Union Status:* No public mention of union affiliation.  

3. **State Optical** (under Europa Eyewear)  
   - *Location:* Illinois  
   - *Known for:* Handmade acetate frames marketed as “luxury” and “artisan-made” in the U.S.  
   - *Union Status:* Not publicly advertised as union.  

4. **Art-Craft Optical**  
   - *Location:* Upstate New York  
   - *Known for:* A very long history of U.S. manufacturing (founded 1918), often sells to the military and safety markets  
   - *Union Status:* While they have a long U.S. manufacturing history, they do not specifically claim union labor.  

5. **Kala Eyewear**  
   - *Location:* California

   - *Union Status:* No publicly indicated union affiliation.  

6. **Lowercase**

---

## 2. Why Union-Made Eyewear is Hard to Find

- **Globalized Industry:** The eyewear industry is heavily globalized. Large manufacturers often have production facilities or supply chains in Italy, China, or Japan where labor costs (and traditional craftsmanship, in some cases) can be advantageous.  
- **Small-Scale Domestic Producers:** Many of the remaining U.S. makers are small artisan or specialty shops that typically don’t belong to a major union.  
- **Specialized Workforce:** Even if production is in the U.S., the facility might not be organized under a union contract. Eyewear assembly can overlap with plastics manufacturing or metalworking, but many such facilities aren’t always unionized.

---

## 3. Tips to Continue the Search

1. **Check the Union Label & Service Trades Department (AFL-CIO) Directory:**  
   - The AFL-CIO’s Union Label & Service Trades Department sometimes lists products (including apparel and accessories) that are union-made in the U.S. While eyewear is not commonly listed, it is worth contacting them directly or checking their most recent directories.

2. **Contact Local Unions in Relevant Industries:**  
   - Eyewear can fall under various labor organizations, such as the International Association of Machinists (IAM), United Steelworkers (USW), or other AFL-CIO affiliates that represent manufacturing workers.  
   - A local union hall (especially in a region known for manufacturing) might know of a smaller shop that happens to be unionized.

3. **Inquire Directly With Small U.S. Factories:**  
   - If you locate a small or niche maker that is proud of its U.S. production, you can email or call to ask about union affiliation. Some companies do not advertise it broadly.

4. **Look for Vintage or NOS (New Old Stock)**  
   - Decades ago, more U.S. eyewear factories were union shops. You may occasionally find vintage frames (e.g., from old Bausch & Lomb, American Optical, or Titmus lines) that were originally produced under a union contract, though verifying this historically can be tricky.

---

### Conclusion

As of now, **there’s no widely recognized, readily available eyeglass frame brand that is openly promoted as *union-made* under the AFL-CIO** in the United States. If having union-made eyewear is essential, your best bet is to:

1. Contact the AFL-CIO’s Union Label department.  
2. Reach out to local manufacturing unions (e.g., IAM, USW).  
3. Investigate smaller U.S. factories directly to see if any of them happen to be union shops.

It can be frustrating that there isn’t a straightforward “union label” for eyeglasses like we see for certain garments, shoes, or tools. But with persistence—particularly contacting the union label department—you may uncover a small-scale operation or a local option that fits your requirements.
