## Assistant

**Mostly true—but with two big caveats.**

Here’s the real rule of thumb for road freezing:

### ✅ **If the *pavement* temperature is above 32°F, the road will not freeze.**
Ground temperature *usually* influences pavement temperature, but not perfectly.

### BUT there are two important exceptions:

---

## **1. Air temperature can drop the pavement temp faster than the ground warms it**
If you get:
- Rapidly falling air temps into the 20s
- Clear skies (radiational cooling)
- Light winds  
…then the pavement can *drop below freezing* even if the ground under it is still 40°F.

This happens a lot on **bridges and shaded roads**.

---

## **2. Precipitation rate matters**
If it’s **raining hard with cold air aloft**, the cold rain can cool the road surface several degrees quickly.

So you can get:
- Ground temp: 35–40°F  
- Pavement temp: 30–31°F  
- Result: **Flash freeze**

This is the setup for black ice mornings in NC.

---

## **Practical NC rule (central Piedmont where you are):**
Here’s what actually works for your area:

### **Roads won’t freeze if:**
- Pavement temp > 32°F  
- Air temp stays above ~28–29°F  
- Precip is light or moderate  
- No strong radiational cooling  

### **Roads *can* freeze even when the ground is above freezing if:**
- Air temp drops **below 25–28°F**, especially quickly  
- Wind is calm and skies are clear overnight  
- A cold rain falls before a hard freeze  
- It’s a bridge, ramp, or elevated road  

---

## **Quick field test (what workers use)**
When you walk outside:
- If **wet asphalt looks dull**, it’s just wet.  
- If it looks like it has a **thin sheen or mirror-like reflection**, assume ice.  
- If it *sounds* slightly crunchy under foot, it's starting to freeze.
