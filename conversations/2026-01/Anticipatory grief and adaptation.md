## 1. Your dad: *what does a son actually do here?*

Your instinct is already correct: **availability without panic**.

Your dad’s heart “beating like a drum” matters. That’s not metaphor. That’s acute stress. The moment of clarity your mom had is especially destabilizing, because it flips roles for a second. She *sees* what’s happening, and that hurts him more than confusion does. It’s like she briefly comes back just to say goodbye again.

What helps him most right now is not problem-solving. It’s **containment**.

Here’s what that looks like in practice:

**1. Be a steady surface, not a rescuer.**  
Say things like:
- “I’m here. You don’t have to hold it together with me.”
- “You’re doing enough. You’re not failing her.”

Avoid:
- “Have you tried…”
- “You should probably…”
Unless he explicitly asks.

**2. Normalize his reaction.**  
Caregivers often feel shame for their physical reactions. Let him know:
- A racing heart is a stress response, not weakness.
- This would rattle *anyone* who loved deeply and stayed.

**3. Offer *specific* help, not open-ended offers.**  
Instead of “Let me know if you need anything,” try:
- “I can take X afternoon this week.”
- “Do you want me to sit with her so you can sleep?”
- “I can handle calls / errands / paperwork for a bit.”

This preserves his dignity. He doesn’t have to *ask*.

**4. Watch for caregiver burnout signals.**  
The big ones:
- Sleep collapse
- Irritability or emotional flatness
- Somatic symptoms (heart, stomach, headaches)

If you see those, your role becomes **advocate**, not son. That might mean gently pushing for respite care, even if he resists. Love sometimes has to override pride.

And one important truth:  
**You cannot shorten this for him.**  
But you *can* make it less lonely. That’s real labor. It counts.

## 2. You: fear of “what happens to my brain”

This is the part where I want to be very clear and grounding, because anxiety loves to lie here.

### First: grief chemistry is real — but it’s temporary.

Yes, when your mom dies, **your nervous system will flood**. Cortisol, adrenaline, sleep disruption, attention disruption. That’s not dementia. That’s **acute grief physiology**.

It looks like:
- Forgetting words
- Misplacing items
- Time distortion
- Mental fog

That is **normal**, **reversible**, and **not predictive**.

### Second: your lifelong forgetfulness is *not* a warning sign.

Everything you described about your childhood and adulthood organization screams **neurodevelopmental pattern**, not neurodegeneration.

Key differences:

**ADHD / autistic-style memory**
- Always there
- Context-dependent
- Improves with structure
- Worse under stress
- “I lose things but remember systems, concepts, meanings”

**Dementia**
- Progressive
- New onset
- Loss of procedural memory
- Loss of meaning, not just objects
- Confabulation or unawareness

You didn’t *start* losing things. You’ve always lost things. You even joke about it. That’s actually protective information. Dementia doesn’t come with lifelong self-awareness of scatter.

Also: grief does **not** cause dementia. There is no evidence for that. Stress can unmask vulnerabilities temporarily, but it does not create neurodegenerative disease.

### Third: what you’re really afraid of

This isn’t about keys or jackets.

It’s about this thought:
> “What if I turn into my mom?”

That’s a terror thought, not a probability.

And here’s the grounding truth:
- You monitor yourself.
- You reflect.
- You ask questions.
- You seek reality checks.

Those are functions that dementia *destroys early*.

Your brain isn’t failing. It’s bracing.

## What *will* happen to you when she dies?

Likely:
- A delayed grief wave (because you already mourned)
- Anger you didn’t expect
- Relief followed by guilt
- Exhaustion

What usually helps:
- **Routine anchoring** (same coffee, same walk, same bed time)
- **External memory supports** (keys always in the same bowl — not because you’re “losing it,” but because grief steals bandwidth)
- **Permission to be slower** for a while

This is not decline. It’s **load**.
