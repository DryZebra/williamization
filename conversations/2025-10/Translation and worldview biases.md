Excellent question — yes, there are several verses where **רוּחַ (ruach)** reveals this exact problem: it’s a word that literally means *breath, wind,* or *spirit*, but which carries *entire theological worlds* inside it. Let’s look at a few key examples and then focus on one where translation choices expose worldview differences.

---

## **1. The Word Itself**
**Hebrew:** רוּחַ (*ruach*)  
**Root Meaning:** wind, breath, spirit, mind, disposition, animating force  

It’s used over **380 times** in the Hebrew Bible — sometimes for wind, sometimes for breath, sometimes for God’s Spirit.  
How translators decide *which sense to use* reveals their worldview about what “spirit” even *is.*

---

## **2. The Example: Genesis 1:2**

> **Hebrew:**  
> וְרוּחַ אֱלֹהִים מְרַחֶפֶת עַל־פְּנֵי הַמָּיִם׃  
> *ve-ruach elohim merachefet al p'nei ha-mayim*  
> “and the **ruach of God** was hovering over the face of the waters.”

### **Translations:**
| Version | Rendering | Theological Implication |
|----------|------------|--------------------------|
| **KJV** | “And the *Spirit of God* moved upon the face of the waters.” | Affirms a *personal* and *active* Spirit of God — consistent with Trinitarian theology. |
| **NRSVUE (2021)** | “A *wind from God* swept over the face of the waters.” | De-personalizes ruach, turning it into a natural *force* rather than divine presence. |
| **JPS Tanakh (Jewish)** | “A *wind from God* sweeping over the water.” | Same as NRSVUE — avoids Christian pneumatology entirely. |
| **NET Bible** | “The *Spirit of God* was moving over the surface of the waters.” | Retains traditional Christian pneumatology. |
| **NABRE (Catholic)** | “A *mighty wind* sweeping over the waters.” | Ambiguous middle ground — poetic, but downplays personality. |

---

### **Why this matters**

This is the **first appearance of God’s Spirit in the Bible**.  
If you render *ruach* as **Spirit**, you’re saying: *the divine presence was actively preparing creation.*  
If you render it as **wind**, you’re saying: *a force or natural phenomenon moved as part of creation.*  

That one decision defines whether creation begins with **a Person** or **a process**.

So, when the NRSVUE (an explicitly “interfaith” translation committee including Jewish scholars) changes “Spirit” to “wind,” they aren’t corrupting anything per se — they’re interpreting reality through a worldview in which the Spirit is *not* an independent divine hypostasis.

It’s a totally predictable, even honest, reflection of bias.  
But it proves your point: **translation can’t escape theology.**
