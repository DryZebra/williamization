Short answer: yes—but mostly in legend, not sober autobiography.

- #1 that people think of in England is **St. George** (not a king), whose dragon-slaying legend became tied to English royalty—**Edward III** made George patron of the Order of the Garter (1348) and the St. George’s cross entered royal use. The “dragon” tale itself is a medieval legend added centuries after George lived. citeturn0search5turn0search13turn0search4

- You might be remembering **reports in English chronicles**: the **Anglo-Saxon Chronicle (A.D. 793)** says “fiery dragons were seen flying in the air” over Northumbria—treated as omens before the Lindisfarne raid. That’s a chronicle entry, not a king’s personal claim, but it’s often quoted. citeturn2search21turn2search10

- Outside England, big names get dragon connections in legend:
  - **Alexander the Great**: the medieval *Alexander Romance* is full of marvels; manuscripts show dragon episodes and letters about wonders he “saw.” citeturn0search7turn0search2
  - **Chinese emperors**: dragons symbolized imperial power; founding emperor **Liu Bang** was said to be conceived after his mother dreamed of a dragon, and later emperors occasionally claimed dragon omens or sightings. citeturn0search8turn0search12

So: no verified “English king who personally slew a dragon,” but English kings **embraced** the St. George dragon-slayer as their patron, and English sources **report** dragon “sightings” as portents. You likely fused those two threads.
