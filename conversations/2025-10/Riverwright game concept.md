# Riverwright: Opening Current

Summer at the edge was a hot silver thing. The fields of Ernst rolled in tidy squares, and beyond them the darker, sterner lines of Perihelion’s border woods held their shape like a vow. Vestnik was eight the first time he saw the Hibernal bannerman crest the ridge, white stag stitched on winter-blue, and the village went quiet the way kind people do when power passes by.

They came every year, the Hibernal cousins, to take the mountain air and pray at the cold spring that locals called the Cathedral’s First Tear. Their nobleman brought a small household—two scribes, a cook, a quiet chaplain, and a maid with a daughter the same age as Vestnik. The girl’s hair was black as wet bark. Her name, he learned later, meant “snowmelt,” the water that moves when winter loses its grip. Until then, she was only the girl who climbed faster than he did.

The maid’s daughter found him in the hedge-shadow behind the chapel ruin, where sun-bleached icons lay broken in the grass like teeth. She did not ask permission to share his shade. She knelt, traced the cracked face of a saint with careful fingers, and said, “They say this one forgave a king.”

Vestnik shrugged—village boys pretend not to wonder. “Kings don’t ask.”

“Maybe not aloud.” She looked at him then, measuring. “Do you belong to them?”

He almost laughed. His father mended harnesses; his mother mended neighbors. They had little to do with kings. “I belong to the river,” he said, meaning the shallow run that fed the mill, meaning the path that carried trout and rumor and everything that moved.

“Good,” she said, as if it settled something, and rose. “If you belong to the river, you can show me where it begins.”

They set off together along the weir, past Ernst fences bright with creosote and the first Perihelion pines, where the air smelled of iron and prayer. The chaplain watched them go and pretended not to see.

They found the spring by sound, a stitched-up hush beneath the hillside—water shouldering its way out of stone. Above it, hammered into a dead cedar, hung an old Perihelion ward: a sun-disk with a split line through it, the sign that once marked a gate you passed only when you were certain. The girl touched the disk and closed her eyes.

“My house keeps the stories,” she whispered. “Yours kept the line.”

He didn’t know what that meant. He knew the village sayings: Perihelion people were steel wrapped in linen; they bowed with their hands on their tools. Hibernal people were winter-true and kinder for it, the keepers of shrines. Leonas soldiers, when they rode through, gleamed like a fact you couldn’t argue. Estival merchants weighed everything and smiled only when the scales matched. Vernal made—everything worth using had their soot on it. Ernst fed them all and prayed with their hats on.

“Do you believe in the betrayer?” she asked, eyes still closed. Children in Perihelion were warned to keep that word off their tongues—too many ears, too many stories with edges. Thirty-five years since the queen had nearly died, since the Perihelion lord was taken and cut before the banners, since their lands were split like bread between Hibernal and Estival holdings, and yet the word still came like a draft through closed doors.

Vestnik chose the river’s answer, the one that let things pass. “I believe in what happened next.”

Her eyes opened. “That sounds like forgiveness.”

“It sounds like work,” he said. “We plant. We mend.” He hesitated, then pointed at the ward. “We remember.”

She smiled—quick, approving. “Then help me remember how to climb.” And she was off again, up the cedar, boots digging for purchase, as if gravity were a suggestion and the world would forgive her for ignoring it. Vestnik followed, because some people are currents and some are stones and, even as a boy, he understood he would rather be moved than left behind.

Halfway up, the cedar cracked. A knife of sound, a sudden lean. He hooked an arm and swung, bark biting his palm. She slipped, heel skidding, and in the panic of the moment he said something he did not mean to say—words he had only ever heard in the mouths of elders after drink and dark.

“By right of Perihelion’s blood.”

It was a scrap of an oath no commoner owned, a phrase from the old watch-songs, the kind that made men straighten and look for witnesses. The girl froze and looked down at him with a new, bright caution.

“Who taught you that?” she asked softly.

“Nobody,” he said, the stupid truth of it ringing as loud as a lie.

They made it down with knees full of sap and a secret neither had asked to carry. At the spring, the chaplain was waiting. His hands were clean; his eyes were not.

“Children,” he said, tone easy as a path. “If you are going to climb dead kings, pray first.”

They laughed because he wanted them to, because laughter is a gate that opens and resets the world. But the chaplain’s gaze lingered on Vestnik a heartbeat too long, as if the boy had walked out of a page he recognized.

Years turned. The girl’s visits came with seasons. They learned each other’s shortcuts and silences, mapped the hill names only locals bothered with, shared bread and small trespasses. When she returned, she brought city words that fit badly in his mouth and left behind the habit of looking at doors as if they had answers. When she left, winter came easier.

On the eve of his eighteenth year, with work scarce and coin thinner, the royal proclamations came riding under the Leonas sun—call for a Selection, an old rite polished new: citizens to present themselves for service, for merit-chosen rise, for the queen’s eye. It was theater. It was bait. It was a ladder set in a square where most men were invited to watch.

Vestnik had woken that morning with a whim like a stone in his shoe. He told himself it was only to see the city, only to touch the forge-bright walls of Vernal’s smith-market, only to stand beneath the Hibernal spires where prayers hung like frost. He told himself it was a lark, a joke he would tell the river when he came home. He wrote a line on a scrap of hide to his mother—“I will be back before the first hay is stacked”—and another to the girl, who by then had become a letter that arrived with scented wax and careful, winter-tight penmanship: “If I see your city, I will find the spring inside it.”

He walked out under a sky the color of clean steel. At the crossroads shrine he paused, because a Perihelion boy pauses, and because the ward in the cedar had never truly left him. He touched the sun-disk split—finger to metal, metal to memory—and, without thinking, he said it again, the old watch-song scrap that did not belong to him:

“By right of Perihelion’s blood.”

The wind shifted. Far down the road, a rider who had been waiting on nothing in particular turned his horse.

Somewhere in the city, a clerk in Hibernal blue set down a cup he did not remember picking up. An old ledger was opened. A name was underlined that no law had written.

And in a shuttered room not far from the palace, where maps pinned with thread crossed the old Perihelion marches like scars, a woman with frost-pale eyes said to the quiet assembled:

“He’s moving.”

# Riverwright: The False Name  

The Selection Square was a roar of banners and brass. Names rang out like hammer blows, each one polished by ancestry, smoothed by a line of fathers and deeds. Vestnik waited at the end of the queue, where the shade of the cathedral spire cut a cool line across the cobbles. When his turn came, the clerk didn’t look up — he just held out his hand.  

“Name and house.”  

Vestnik opened his mouth. Nothing. The people of the marches didn’t *have* surnames. They had trades and weather scars and graves, not pedigrees.  

He said, “Vestnik, of the River,” because silence would mean the end of it, and that phrase had always been enough for home.  

The clerk misheard. His quill scratched. “Vestnik Perihelion,” he muttered, and the ink bled like a wound.  

The word hung there, royal and forbidden. The air itself seemed to hold its breath.  

By the time Vestnik realized what had happened, the seal was pressed, the parchment rolled. The clerk looked up, brow arched, curiosity caught between duty and disbelief. “That name,” he said, low. “I thought it was dust.”  

Vestnik swallowed. “So did I.”

Later, among the relic shelves, he found it — an old volume bound in cracked white leather, stamped with the mark of the **Order of the Photon.** The paladins’ lineage — the first light-bringers sworn to serve the crown and the Church before the schism, before the betrayal.  

He turned the brittle pages, tracing lines of genealogy and scripture. Each family had sigils, records, oaths. Then, near the end, a section torn out. Whole lines blacked over with ink. The **House of Perihelion** listed once — then crossed out, the symbol of the sun cleaved in two.  

But the Order’s creed caught his eye:  
> “The Light is no crown, but a reflection.  
>  Those who bear its weight are neither chosen nor denied,  
>  only found where it shines back.”  

He read it again, slower this time. *Found where it shines back.*  

Something in the phrasing was wrong — or rather, not wrong, but *deliberate*. An older translation, maybe. Or a cipher.  

As he left the temple, bells rang again — but softer this time, as though the city itself were watching. He carried the book beneath his coat, not knowing that two figures from the inner cloister followed him with their eyes — one priestess, one knight — and that the clerk who had written his name had already been called to account.  

That night, in a borrowed loft above a Vernal smithy, he opened the book again. Beneath the inked-out names, faint impressions of the letters remained. The hand that had tried to erase them had pressed too hard.  

He rubbed charcoal over the surface and blew away the dust. Letters surfaced like bones.  

**O T H O  P  H O T O N I S** — not a name, but a title. A forgotten suborder.  

And beneath that:  
> *“Those who carried no name, but bore the light.”*  

His pulse quickened. For the first time, his lack of a name didn’t feel like absence. It felt like inheritance.
