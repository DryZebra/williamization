1. **“Casting out demons in your name” as metaphor of doing good in your tradition**

3. **“Not against us is for us” — a principle of minimal positive recognition**  
   In human social and political movements, you often see this principle: we may disagree on non-essential methods, but if you are not actively opposing the cause, you count as an ally. It’s a generous principle: you don’t require full alignment to be a collaborator.  

4. **Small acts count**  
   Jesus’ example of “a cup of water” reminds us: big gestures, grand heroics, matter less than consistent, small, caring acts. From a materialist perspective, this grounds spirituality in everyday kindness, not supernatural miracles.

5. **Reward as social/social-psychological rather than cosmic**  
   The “reward” promised could be read not as celestial credit, but as the emergent social fruit: trust, relational capital, community strengthening, moral reputation, increased cooperation. ### Section 3: Historical Continuity — Outsiders Who Cast Out Demons

We have heard the Lord’s word: *“Forbid him not … for he that is not against us is for us.”* We have seen the measure change from membership to fruit. Now we must ask the question that tests every doctrine: has this proved true in history? Did the Lord’s own rule stand when the Church went out into the world? Or did it collapse under the weight of time? If Christ’s word is living, then it will be found living in the record of human deeds. We will not trade in guesses. We will look at the fruit.

#### 1) A biblical precedent that establishes the pattern, not a loophole (Numbers 11)
Before we step into the centuries, we mark the Scripture that Christ’s action echoes. In the wilderness, the Spirit rested on Eldad and Medad though they were not standing in the tent with the seventy. Joshua, zealous for Moses, said, “My lord Moses, forbid them.” And Moses answered, “Enviest thou for my sake? would God that all the LORD’s people were prophets.” The logic is the same: when God’s work is visible, jealousy must fall silent. The point is not that “anyone can do whatever they please,” but that when the true work appears, it must be received as God’s work even if it breaks our lines. That pattern is not poetry; it is instruction. Mark shows Christ doing what Moses longed to see.

#### 2) Abolition — when Christians named the demon and others joined the exorcism
Centuries later, the Church faced the old world’s strongest principality: human bondage. Christians did not discover slavery as a fact; they denounced it as a sin. Quakers labored against it for generations; John Wesley wrote and preached against it; revivalists like Charles Finney refused the Lord’s table to slaveholders; the Black church, born in suffering, witnessed that God is the liberator of captives. In Britain, a Christian statesman, William Wilberforce, fought the slave trade until it fell. The slogans of the movement were the language of Scripture: justice rolling down like waters, love casting out fear, the image of God in every neighbor. #### 5) The test of truth in these histories
In each of these scenes, the pattern of Mark 9 is visible. First, the Church names the evil and bears the cost of naming it. The man in Mark 9 is not doing tricks by a strange spell; he is casting out demons *in Jesus’ name*. That is the condition the Lord Himself lays down. History’s examples follow it: where the Church named the work, and others consented to that name, the work was counted as ours.
