entity["people", "Vladimir Lenin", 0]’s use of the term **“Great-Russian chauvinism”** (in Russian, *velikorusskiy shovinizm*) refers to a specific kind of nationalist and imperialist attitude by the dominant Russian (or “Great Russian”) element within the former Russian Empire and early Soviet Union. In brief: he saw it as a major threat to proletarian internationalism, to the equality of nations and to the stability of the multi‐national state he wished to build. ### What did Lenin mean by “Great-Russian chauvinism”?  
Here are the key elements:

1. **Dominant-nation Nationalism**  
   Lenin used the term to describe the ideology of a dominant ethnic group (here: Great Russians) claiming superiority and exercising dominance over other nationalities. In his own words: he denounced the “Really Russian man, the Great-Russian chauvinist … in substance a rascal and a tyrant, such as the typical Russian bureaucrat is.” citeturn0search7turn0search0turn0search4  
   In other words: a mentality of “we Russians are the main/true nation; the others are lesser, subject, or need to be assimilated.”

2. **Imperial / Colonial Practice**  
   It wasn’t just about cultural pride, but about structural domination of non-Russian nationalities (Ukrainians, Georgians, Finns, Baltic peoples, etc.) by Russian institutions, language policy, bureaucracy, and the state. Lenin linked “Great-Russian chauvinism” with the old Tsarist empire’s oppression of minorities (“prison of the peoples”). citeturn0search4turn0search0turn0search21  
   He treated it as a legacy of imperial domination that the Bolshevik revolution needed to break with.

3. **Threat to Unity of the Working Class & of the State**  
   Lenin argued that allowing or tolerating Great-Russian chauvinism would undermine the solidarity of proletarians of different nationalities, encourage resentment, and thereby weaken the multi‐national socialist state. For example, the bureaucratic dominance of Russians in party or state organs could drown out non-Russian workers:  
   > “the infinitesimal percentage of Soviet and sovietised workers will drown in that tide of chauvinistic Great-Russian riffraff like a fly in milk.” citeturn0search7turn0search0  
   So it’s not just a moral critique, but a practical one: for socialist strategy and the survival of the union of republics.

4. **Right of Self-Determination & Language/Culture Rights**  
   Because of his concern about Great-Russian chauvinism, Lenin backed the right of oppressed nationalities to self-determination, to their own language, to schools, to autonomy — at least in principle. For example he criticized policies that forced Russian language on non-Russians: “you cannot drive people to heaven with an oak-wood club.” citeturn0search4turn0search21  
   He framed the national question, in part, as one of giving genuine equality to non-Russian peoples so that the working class of all nations could unite.

5. **Distinguishing Patriotism from Chauvinism**  
   Lenin said that a sense of national pride among Great Russias (e.g., the Russian proletariat) was not in itself wrong: for example, in one essay he said “Is a sense of national pride alien to us, Great-Russian class-conscious proletarians? Certainly not!” citeturn0search0  
   But he distinguished this from the *chauvinism* of claiming dominance or suppressing other nations. So the key is: **class-conscious national pride** vs **national oppression/imperialism**.

### Why it was important in Lenin’s thinking  
- Since the old Russian Empire encompassed many non-Russian nationalities (Ukrainians, Poles, Finns, Baltic peoples, Caucasians, Central Asians), Lenin saw the national question as central to revolution and state-building. citeturn0search18  
- He believed that if Bolshevik power simply replaced Tsarist rule but retained the same domination of non-Russians by Russians, the revolution would betray itself—it would regress back into imperialism rather than overcome it.  
- In his later years he warned that the main danger to the Soviet Union (or a future multinational socialist union) was less “local nationalisms” than the national oppression of minority peoples by the dominant Russian nation. citeturn0search11  
- He hoped that by promoting equality of nations and reversing national oppression, the working class everywhere would be more likely to unite voluntarily, rather than be divided along national lines.

### Some critical nuances and contradictions  
- While Lenin condemned forced assimilation (e.g., forcing Russian language), he also sometimes said that assimilation of the Russian and Ukrainian proletariat was “an incontestable fact… and undoubtedly a progressive thing”—which many critics see as contradictory. citeturn0search4turn0search21  
- Some historians argue that in practice, the Soviet state still ended up centralised and Russian-dominated, so the ideal of overcoming Great-Russian chauvinism was never fully realised. citeturn0search12turn0search19  
- The term “chauvinism” itself is strong: Lenin was not merely objecting to Russians being proud of their country, but to a dominant-nation ideology that oppressed other peoples.

### In sum  
So, in one sentence: Lenin’s point about “Great-Russian chauvinism” was that in a multi-national state with a socialist ambition, the dominance of one nation (Russians) over others threatened both the equality of nations and the solidarity of the working class — hence it had to be actively combated, and genuine national equality upheld.
