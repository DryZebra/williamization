Short answer: yep—quite a few.

**Where the “gas-pipe DNA” still shows up today**

- **Conduit trade sizes & threads.** RMC/IMC (and the fittings that go with them—locknuts, couplings, Myers hubs, etc.) still use the old iron-pipe trade sizes and **NPT** threads, inherited directly from gas/plumbing practice. That’s why ½, ¾, 1 in., etc. conduit sizes don’t match actual ODs, and why you can cut RMC threads on a pipe die. citeturn0search3turn0search12

- **Knockouts and box/fitting dimensions.** The diameters stamped into boxes and enclosures are sized to those same trade sizes, so the ecosystem of bushings, chase nipples, and KO seals all map back to the iron-pipe sizing system. (Industry design guides still describe conduit ODs as being driven by **IPS—Iron Pipe Size**.) citeturn0search18turn0search3

- **Lighting hardware threads (“lamp pipe”).** The lighting world still uses **1/8-27 IPS** (and related IPS sizes) for hollow stems, nipples, and canopies—originally meant to pass gas to a burner, later reused to pass wires. Open any modern chandelier kit and you’ll see 1/8-27 IPS parts. citeturn0search11turn0search1

- **Hybrid lineage in fixtures.** Early “combination” gas-electric chandeliers (“gasoliers”) are the historical bridge—gas pipe carrying the frame with electric conductors snaked through. Reproduction and antique markets preserve the form factor that today’s lamp hardware still fits. citeturn0search4turn0search9

**Context note:** In the very early electrification era, it was common to **reuse existing gas piping as a raceway** (especially up to ceiling outlets). That practice is long out of code, but it explains why our electrical hardware dimensions still look suspiciously like plumbing. citeturn0search15turn0search5
