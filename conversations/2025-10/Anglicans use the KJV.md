Many Anglicans **do use the King James Version (KJV)** — but it’s not universal.

Here’s how it breaks down:

### 🕍 **Church of England (UK)**
- The **KJV (Authorized Version, 1611)** was originally commissioned by King James *for* the Church of England.
- It remains a **foundational text** and is still used for:
  - Formal liturgies
  - Choral Evensong
  - State occasions and cathedrals
- However, in most **parish churches**, modern translations are now more common, such as:
  - **New Revised Standard Version (NRSV)**
  - **New International Version (NIV)**
  - **Common Worship lectionary readings** (which often use the NRSV)

### 🇺🇸 **Episcopal Church (Anglican in the U.S.)**
- Officially authorizes several translations, including:
  - **NRSV** (preferred)
  - **KJV**
  - **RSV**, **NIV**, and others for teaching or study
- The **KJV is still allowed** for public reading, especially in traditionalist or Anglo-Catholic parishes.

### 🕊️ **Continuing / Traditional Anglicans**
- Many **Continuing Anglican** or **Anglican Catholic** churches (e.g., ACC, APA, TAC) **still use the KJV exclusively**, often with the **1928 Book of Common Prayer**, because they see it as doctrinally and stylistically faithful.

- **Yes, Anglicans use the KJV**, especially in traditional contexts.
- **Modern Anglican churches** often use the **NRSV** or **NIV** for readability and inclusive language.

Excellent question — and it’s one of those things that makes sense once you see the history in motion.  

---

### ⚔️ **The Short Version**
**“Continuing Anglicans”** are churches that **broke away from the official Anglican Communion** (like the Church of England or Episcopal Church USA) in the **1970s onward**, in order to *continue* traditional Anglicanism — especially its older theology, liturgy, and morality.

They call themselves “Continuing” because they believe *they* are continuing the true faith and practice of classical Anglicanism, even though they left the official structures.

---

### 🕰️ **How It Started**
In **1976**, the **Episcopal Church (USA)** began major theological and liturgical reforms:
- Adopted the **1979 Book of Common Prayer** (modernized, changed doctrine of Eucharist and baptism)
- **Ordained women as priests and bishops**
- Began moving toward **liberal theology** (inclusive language, moral reinterpretations)

Many clergy and laity rejected these changes and formed a movement called the **Continuing Anglican Movement**.  
They organized in **1977** at the **Congress of St. Louis**, writing a declaration that rejected the new reforms and reaffirmed:
- The **Thirty-Nine Articles**
- The **1928 Book of Common Prayer**
- Traditional male priesthood
- The **King James Version** as their normative English Bible.

---

### ⛪ **Major Continuing Anglican Bodies Today**
All are independent but in communion with one another to varying degrees:
- **Anglican Catholic Church (ACC)**
- **Anglican Province of America (APA)**
- **Traditional Anglican Church (TAC)**
- **Anglican Church in America (ACA)**
- **Reformed Episcopal Church (REC)** (older split, 1873, but often allied)

Most of them:
- Use the **1928 Book of Common Prayer** (U.S.) or **1662 BCP** (England)
- Use the **KJV** for Scripture readings
- Celebrate liturgy in **Elizabethan English**
- Retain traditional views on marriage, gender, and morality

If the modern Episcopal Church is the *evolution* of Anglicanism,
