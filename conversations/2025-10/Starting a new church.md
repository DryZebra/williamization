## 1. Objections, doubts, and starting assumptions

You already sense some of these, and rightly so. Let’s name them and see how they can be confronted.

### “Everyone says I can’t, because I’m not Christian (or not orthodox).”

- Some churches *will* reject you for theological reasons. That’s their prerogative. But you don’t have to start under an existing church or denomination. You can start a new one (or a fresh expression) with your own theological commitments (so long as you are honest).
- The barrier is more social and reputational than legal (in the U.S.). You will have to win trust, demonstrate integrity, show depth, and build relationships. That usually takes time.

### “How many pastors sin daily?”

- A lot. Pastors are human, and many fail, struggle, fall short. That does not disqualify the vocation, though it means you must develop character, accountability, humility, and self-care.
- The presence of failure in many churches is part of why some people feel disillusioned, which is also part of your push for “deeper” or more honest Christianity.

### “Why is there no church for people like me?”

- There *are* many non-denominational, “progressive,” “emergent,” “house church,” “semi-academic” Christian groups that try to push deeper theology, community, apostolic thinking, etc. It’s rare to find one exactly matching your style, but that doesn’t mean it doesn’t exist.
- Some of them are experimental, marginal, or informal, so you might have to build your own.

So your frustration is legit. The question is: can you *be* the thing you want to see? And that means not just dreaming but doing—step by step.

## 2. What does “being a pastor / church leader” *mean*?

Before you start, clarify what *you* mean by “pastor.” Here are common duties and expectations, and you will need to choose (or reject) them:

- Preaching / teaching — regularly delivering spiritual content, exegesis, theology, application.
- Pastoral care — counseling, visiting the sick, helping people spiritually and emotionally.
- Leadership / vision / administration — governance, decision making, vision casting, oversight.
- Community formation — discipleship, small groups, mentoring, equipping others.
- Sacramental / ritual / worship leadership — depending on your theology, leading baptisms, communion/ordinances, worship services, liturgy, etc.

Your version might lean more heavily on the “depth, history, theology” side, less on the sentimental or anecdotal, which is fine — but you’ll still need to do the relational, administrative, and pastoral (caring people) side.

Also: a “pastor” is not always the same as a “paid, full-time minister.” Many pastors begin part-time, as volunteers, or alongside another job.

## 3. What you’ll need (spiritual, intellectual, personal foundation)

Before launching something new, build a foundation in these areas:

### A. Theological / historical / biblical depth

If you’re looking for *depth*, make that a core of your life now:

- Study church history, early church fathers, the Reformation, theology, biblical scholarship.
- Read widely: modern theology, philosophy of religion, scholarship, criticisms, apologetics, etc.
- Engage in your own spiritual formation — prayer, meditation, scripture, but also wrestle with doubts.

Without depth, you risk being shallow or inconsistent.

### B. Character, relational maturity, accountability

- Develop humility, patience, integrity, emotional maturity.
- Find mentors or peers who will challenge you, call you out, support you.
- Practice preaching, teaching, leading small groups, doing pastoral care in small ways now (volunteer, small group leader, etc.)

### C. Experience in “church / spiritual community”

Even if you don’t like many churches, you probably need *some* immersion in a community that at least partially aligns with your values—so you see how things work (good and bad). That gives social insight, institutional insight, relational networks.

### D. Clarify your vision, identity, theology

Write down: What is your “faith expression”? What are your non-negotiables? What is your “creed” (in your own terms)? What does worship look like? How do you understand sacraments or ordinances? What kind of community do you want to build?

This becomes your “platform” or “distinctive.” If your theology is vague or shifting, people will hesitate to trust.

## 5. Surviving, attracting people, income, sustainability

This is the practical “how you don’t starve or disappear” part.

### Attracting people / building a congregation

- **Start small** — it’s easier to build deep commitment with 10–30 people than chase 300.  
- **Serve a niche / need** — what unmet spiritual hunger or intellectual gap are you filling? Appeal to people who want depth, theology, honest struggle, history, etc.  
- **Quality teaching / content** — your strength (depth) can be a differentiator. Record sermons, publish essays, podcast, share on social, draw an online audience.  
- **Relational outreach** — invite friends, host discussion groups, community service, shared meals, small groups.  
- **Consistency** — regular meeting times, dependable schedule helps people trust.  
- **Partnerships / networks** — collaborate with other churches, organizations, speakers, local ministries.

### Income & finances

- Many pastors in new churches start *bi-vocational* (having another income job) while the church is small.  
- Encourage **donations / tithes / offerings** (once you have tax-exempt status).  
- Offer services (teaching, workshops, retreats, classes) which people can pay for.  
- Grants, mission partnerships, philanthropy (if aligned).  
- Be extremely frugal early: minimal overhead, volunteer staff, shared spaces rather than owning property.  
- Think creatively: digital products (books, courses), online community subscriptions, crowdfunding, etc.

### Risks and pitfalls

- Burnout (pastoring is emotionally taxing).  
- Conflict, leadership failures, relational breakdowns.  
- Lawsuits, liability, financial mismanagement.  
- Theological drift or internal division.  
- Regret if you don’t have sustainable finances.

2. **Immerse yourself in study & mentorship**

   Read deeply in church history, theology, biblical criticism. Find mentors (pastors, professors, older Christians) who will challenge you. 3. **Practice in small ways**

   Start a small group, lead a discussion series, preach (or teach) occasionally to friends, blog or podcast on theology. Build a track record.

4. **Begin gathering a “core group”**

   Talk to people (friends, seekers, skeptics) who might want what you want. Start meeting informally (in homes, parks, coffee shops). Test your content, your style, your community.

5. **Legal and business prep**

   Choose your name, look at state laws for nonprofits in your area, talk with a nonprofit / ministry lawyer (or someone who helps churches). Draft bylaws, articles of incorporation, plan your governance model.

6. **Soft launch / pilot phase**

   After some months of building relationships, begin a “preview launch” meeting (worship, teaching, Q&A, gathering) to a small invited crowd. Use it to test your processes: hospitality, worship flow, feedback, adjustments.

7. **Iterate, commit, grow**

   Learn from every gathering, ask for feedback, refine the experience, keep doing relationships, keep inviting people. Once you have some consistency, open it more publicly.
