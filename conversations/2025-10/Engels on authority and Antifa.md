---

### 1. Engels’ Argument in Context

Engels wrote *On Authority* (1872) as a response to the Bakuninist anarchists who rejected all forms of authority. His central point was **authority is not inherently oppressive — it is a necessary expression of collective coordination in production and struggle.**

> “A revolution is certainly the most authoritarian thing there is; it is the act whereby one part of the population imposes its will upon the other part by means of rifles, bayonets and cannon.”

Engels is not glorifying domination — he’s saying *the abolition of authority is impossible while material coordination requires it.*  
When a society or a movement pretends authority can be abolished, it simply **re-emerges in hidden or hypocritical forms** — often more reactionary and less accountable than open, material authority.

---

### 2. Applying This to the “Antifa” Paradox

Many modern liberal “anti-fascist” movements operate under **idealized spontaneity** — no hierarchy, no central command, no party discipline. In practice, however, this vacuum of legitimate authority invites **informal hierarchies, media gatekeepers, NGO funding networks, and intelligence infiltration** to fill the gap.  

What results is not the abolition of authority but its **privatization** — power without accountability.

Thus, Engels’ warning plays out perfectly:  
> “In all social combinations of men, a certain authority is necessary, which is accepted and recognized by all.”

When that necessary authority is denied, the movement becomes directionless, emotional, and easy to manipulate. “Antifa” becomes, in effect, **the antithesis of itself** — a *reactionary* movement under the banner of anti-reaction.
