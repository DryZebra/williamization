Why that one line is risky
- It’s **directive and mildly moralizing**: “You don’t have to murder this person today” tells them what *not* to do and implicitly calls them a murderer — that can increase defensiveness or trigger anger. Negotiation doctrine warns against moralizing or lecturing before rapport exists. citeturn1search4  
- It **assumes you’ve already built influence**. The FBI’s Behavioral Change Stairway Model (BCSM) stresses you must move from active listening → empathy → rapport → influence before you can effectively tell someone what to do. Jumping to influence too early often fails. citeturn0search5turn1search5  
- It may be **ambiguous or minimizing**. If the subject thinks killing is the only exit, a flippant “not today” could be interpreted as dismissive of their perceived crisis and make them act sooner to prove resolve.

Why it *could* sometimes help (if used right)
- The phrasing contains a **temporal separation** (“today”) — that can sometimes *de-escalate urgency* by implying a delay is possible (which buys negotiators time). But that effect depends entirely on tone, relationship, and what came before. Time is one of the negotiator’s most valuable tools. citeturn0search2turn0search10

2. **Label and mirror; show empathy (don’t lecture).**

Good — one minute is brutal but there *are* evidence-backed ways to use that time. Below I’ll give you a very short, practical script (30–60s), a handful of single lines you can drop in, and exactly what to *avoid*. Speak slowly, calmly, and quietly — your tone matters more than any single word.

## 60-second script (read as slow, steady, empathetic)
1. (Label) “It sounds like you feel trapped and out of options.”  
2. (Pause 1–2s to let them confirm or correct.)  
3. (Calibrated question + buy time) “If you can give me just one minute, what would you want to happen next so you don’t have to do something you’ll regret?”  
4. (If they answer with reasons) Mirror and validate: “I hear that — feeling trapped, wanting people to listen. I want the same thing for you: a way out that doesn’t end in loss. Can you tell me one thing that would help you step back for the next minute?”  
5. (Close for the short delay) “Okay — thank you. Give me this minute. I’m listening. Tell me the first thing on your mind.”  

Why this works in a crisis minute:
- Labels and mirroring lower immediate emotional heat and keep them talking rather than acting.  
- A calibrated “If you can give me one minute…” asks for a specific, short commitment they can reasonably agree to and converts urgency into time.  
- Asking them to *describe* an alternative makes them mentally rehearse non-violent options (cognitive dissonance vs. their “only way out” belief).  
- Silence after your line forces them to fill the space; people in crisis often talk themselves out of violence when given a listening ear.

## Short one-liners you can use if you can’t do the whole script
- “Can you give me 60 seconds so I can try to help?”  
- “I don’t want to judge — I want to help you find another way. Tell me one thing that would make you stop for a minute.”  
- “Help me understand what would have to change so you didn’t feel you had to do this.”  
- “If you let them live for just one minute, I’ll listen to everything you want people to know.”  
- (If they’re very agitated) “You sound terrified — tell me what scares you most right now.”

## Tone & delivery tips (critical)
- Slow down. Use pauses. Lower your pitch slightly.  
- Use short sentences, no sarcasm. Match their emotional intensity (don’t be breezy).  
- After asking a question — shut up. Let them answer.  
- Repeat key words they use to show you’re listening (“You said ‘no way out’ — tell me more about that.”)

## What *not* to say (in 1-minute scenarios)
- Don’t moralize: avoid “You don’t have to murder…” or “You’re not a monster” — those can escalate or sound accusatory.  
- Don’t threaten or issue ultimatums.  
- Don’t give impossible promises (e.g., “I’ll get you a plane and a pardon”) — only offer what your team can plausibly do.  
- Don’t bargain with punishments (“If you don’t stop I’ll...”) — that removes control from the rapport process.

## If they explicitly say “I’ll kill them now” or you detect imminence
- Get an explicit short commitment: “If you feel like you might do it *now*, tell me one small thing that would stop you for the next 30 seconds.”  
- Even a tiny temporal window (30s–60s) is worth fighting for — it lets you keep talking and lets tactical options move.
