## What “time feeling faster” usually means (in psychology/neuroscience)

First, let me clarify: time itself (in physics) doesn’t change in our daily life. What changes is our *perception* of time — how fast (or slow) our consciousness experiences its passage. The brain is not a perfect clock; it’s a storyteller, interpreter, and filter.

Here are some of the major theories and findings about why time can *feel* like it’s speeding up.

### 1. **Memory density / novelty hypothesis**

One of the most common ideas is that when we have more *novel* experiences — new places, new people, new challenges — our brain lays down more distinct memories. When we later look back, that interval seems longer because it's “richer” in distinct events.

Conversely, if days are filled with routine, predictability, repetition — fewer surprises — then fewer unique memory “anchors” get stored. In retrospect, it feels like that time “collapsed” or merged together. citeturn0search22turn0search7turn0search13turn0search0

So, one metaphor: imagine reading a book full of unexpected plot twists vs reading a repeated, monotonous text. The twist-filled book *feels* longer (fuller) when you reflect back.

Your description of “just sitting, experiencing the excruciating second” is interesting in this context — you are not filling the moment with novelty, but perhaps by deeply attending to each moment, you are *creating* more vivid memory contrast, or resisting the blending into monotony.

### 2. **Attention and prospective vs retrospective time**

How we perceive time *as it happens* (“prospective” time) is different from how we judge a period *afterwards* (“retrospective” time). citeturn0search2turn0search14

- If you pay close attention to time passing (watching the clock, listening to ticks, being very aware), that can *slow down* the subjective sense of time (you notice more “ticks” you’d otherwise gloss over). citeturn0search2turn0search7  
- But when your attention is elsewhere or distracted, moments can slip by unnoticed, and retrospectively they seem shorter.

Because you *deliberately* focus on the passing — “experiencing the excruciating second” — you may be amplifying a mode of time awareness that many don’t practice, which puts you in a different perceptual regime.

### 3. **Slowing of neural processing / “fewer frames per second”**

Some theories propose that as we age (or via changes in the brain), neural processing becomes a little slower (or less information per second), so we effectively sample fewer “frames” of time, making it *feel* as though time is passing faster. citeturn0search5turn0search24turn0search10turn0search14turn0search16

One analogy: in a flipbook, if you draw fewer frames for a given duration, the motion seems quicker. If the brain processes fewer “moments” per unit objective time, that might speed up subjective passage in some sense. However, there’s a tension: if processing slows, you'd expect things to *drag*, not speed up. So this theory is more of a metaphor than a fully fleshed-out explanation.

### 4. **Aging, proportions, and diminishing returns**

There’s a classic “proportional theory”: as you age, each unit of time (one year, one month) becomes a smaller fraction of your total life. To a 10-year-old, a year is 10% of their life; to a 50-year-old, a year is 2%. So psychologically, it “feels” less significant, shorter, etc. citeturn0search22turn0search1turn0search13turn0search14

This doesn’t fully explain the *moment-to-moment* feeling of acceleration, but helps explain the feeling of entire months/years compressing.

### 5. **Emotional, arousal, and dopamine effects**

Emotions and internal arousal states influence time perception. In high-adrenaline or fear states, people often report time slowing down (because more information is processed or because memory encoding is enhanced). citeturn0search12turn0search14turn0search2turn0search19

Dopamine has been tied into models of an “internal clock.” If dopamine signaling is higher, the internal clock might “tick” faster, making objective time feel stretched. On the flip side, when you're more relaxed or your attention drifts, you might “miss” ticks. citeturn0search12

### 6. **Temporal illusions and momentary distortions**

There are known illusions of time, like **chronostasis** (the stopped-clock illusion) — where, right after your eyes make a quick movement (a saccade), the first thing you look at appears to last longer than it does. citeturn0search38

Also, our perception of time is not always uniform or linear: the brain sometimes *fills in gaps* or reconstructs durations based on context, expectation, and prior patterns.
