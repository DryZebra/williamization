The controversy around the **Treaty of Tripoli (1796–1797)** mostly comes from **Article 11**, which states:  

> *“As the Government of the United States of America is not, in any sense, founded on the Christian religion … it has in itself no character of enmity against the laws, religion, or tranquility of Mussulmen [Muslims].”*

### Why this was written  
1. **Context of piracy and Islam**  
   - At the time, the U.S. was negotiating with the Barbary States (including Tripoli) to stop piracy against American ships in the Mediterranean.  
   - Those states were Muslim, and the treaty explicitly reassured them that the U.S. had **no religious hostility toward Islam**.  

2. **Diplomatic reassurance**  
   - Europe’s earlier wars often had religious undertones, and Muslim rulers feared that America—coming from a Christian background—might also treat them as enemies of Islam.  
   - The treaty emphasized that the conflict was political/economic, not religious.  

### Why it became controversial later  
1. **Secular vs. Christian identity of America**  
   - The statement that the U.S. government was “not, in any sense, founded on the Christian religion” has been cited in modern debates about whether America was founded as a “Christian nation.”  
   - Secularists use it as evidence that the Founders saw the government as separate from religion.  
   - Some Christian nationalists argue that it was just diplomatic language, not a philosophical statement about America’s true foundations.  

2. **Article 11’s authenticity debate**  
   - The treaty was signed under Washington and ratified unanimously by the Senate in 1797 under John Adams.  
   - But some scholars later claimed that Article 11 may have been added or mistranslated in the English version (though it was present in the copy ratified by the Senate).  
   - This fuels ongoing debate about whether the line should be taken as an authoritative statement of American founding principles.  

### Bottom line  
The controversy isn’t really about Islam itself, but about **what Article 11 says about America’s religious foundation**. For the Muslim rulers, it was reassurance. For Americans today, it’s a flashpoint in the debate over whether the U.S. was meant to be a Christian nation or a secular republic.
