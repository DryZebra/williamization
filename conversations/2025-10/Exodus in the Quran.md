## Assistant

The *Book of Exodus* itself is not in the Qur’an as a single, unified text like it is in the Bible. But the Qur’an does include many of the same stories and themes that appear in Exodus—especially the story of Moses (*Mūsā*) and the liberation of the Israelites from Pharaoh.

Here’s the breakdown:

- **Moses in the Qur’an**: Moses is the most frequently mentioned prophet in the Qur’an. His story is spread across many chapters (sūrahs), including *al-Baqarah* (2), *al-A‘rāf* (7), *Ṭāhā* (20), *ash-Shu‘arā’* (26), and *al-Qaṣaṣ* (28).

- **Parallels to Exodus**:
  - Moses’ birth and being placed in a basket on the river (similar to Exodus 2).
  - His confrontation with Pharaoh and the magicians.
  - The ten plagues are not listed the same way, but several punishments (blood, locusts, frogs) are mentioned (Qur’an 7:133).
  - The parting of the sea and the drowning of Pharaoh’s army (Qur’an 26:63–66).
  - The receiving of the commandments on Mount Sinai (Qur’an 7:142–145).
  - The Israelites worshiping the golden calf (Qur’an 7:148–150, 20:85–97).

- **Differences**:
  - The Qur’an does not present the narrative in one continuous book—it retells episodes for teaching and moral lessons.
  - Certain details differ. For example, in the Qur’an Pharaoh himself is drowned, whereas in some Jewish interpretations he survives.
  - The Qur’an emphasizes monotheism and obedience to God as the moral of these stories, rather than giving Israel a national law code like Exodus does.

So in short: **Exodus isn’t “in” the Qur’an as a book, but much of its content—especially the Moses and Pharaoh story—is retold across multiple surahs.**

---

## 2. Christianity’s relationship to this kind of material  
Unlike Judaism and Islam, Christianity **does not have a separate “book of law” that governs war, slaves, or captives.** The New Testament does not give Christians their own legal system the way the Torah does for Israel or the Hadith does for Muslims. Instead:  
- The New Testament assumes Roman law is the governing code, and tells Christians to **endure within it while embodying Christ’s ethic** (e.g., Philemon, Ephesians 6:5–9, Colossians 4:1).  
- Captivity and slavery are acknowledged realities, but the emphasis shifts toward **radical equality in Christ**: “There is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus” (Gal. 3:28).  

There is **no “Book of Captives” in Christianity** because Christianity is not a political state in its founding stage. Christians did not receive a Mosaic-style code about how to run wars, distribute captives, or structure an economy.  

---

## 3. What *does* exist that parallels it?  
There are a few places where you see **Christian rules of war / captives**—but they are not biblical, they come later in Christian history:  

- **Augustine’s “Just War” theory** (4th–5th century): laid down moral criteria for when Christians can fight, though not rules for captives.  
- **Canon Law (medieval period):** sometimes addressed ransoming captives, forbidding enslavement of Christians, or regulating treatment of non-Christian prisoners.  
- **Crusader and monastic rules:** orders like the Hospitallers and Templars created practical rules for dealing with Muslim captives, often mirroring Islamic and Jewish codes in some ways.  
- **Theologians like Aquinas:** elaborated on Augustine, stressing that war must be defensive, captives should not be mistreated, and slavery was tolerated only as part of the fallen world.  

---

## 4. Materialist-Christian framing (to give you an edge)  
- The Torah’s laws (like Exodus 21) represent **proto-regulation** inside a brutal economy. They were steps away from total arbitrariness, not the moral finish line.  
- Islam codified slavery with protections that softened it but still left it intact.  
- Christianity, lacking state power at its beginning, **did not legislate slavery—but instead undercut it theologically by making slaves brothers in Christ.** That’s why, centuries later, slavery *collapsed first in Christian societies* (though often through contradictions and class struggle).

- Yes, Exodus reflects a Bronze Age legal code.  
- But Christianity never adopted Exodus 21 as binding. Instead, the New Testament reframed slavery by asserting the spiritual equality of master and slave.  
- Later Christian societies fought bloody wars over this very contradiction (Civil War, abolition), showing the text was not static but revolutionary over time.
