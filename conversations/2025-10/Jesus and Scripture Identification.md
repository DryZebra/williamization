## What “identifying Scripture” means, and what Jesus *didn't* do

- Jesus never directly quotes or refers to *New Testament* writings as “Scripture” (since they were mostly written afterward).  
- He consistently uses the Jewish Scriptures known in His day.  
- He doesn’t treat the Scriptures as optional or symbolic, but as binding and true.  
- Yet, He comes to *fulfill* them — meaning He brings them to their proper meaning, purpose, and completion (not to abolish them). citeturn1search18turn1search14turn1search28turn1search5

## Common “gotcha” traps & how to respond

1. **“Jesus never used the word ‘Bible’”**  
   — True, He didn’t say “the Bible” (that term came much later). But He consistently cited the Scriptures available to Him and treated them as God’s authoritative and unbreakable Word.  

2. **“What about extra-biblical sayings or oral tradition?”**  
   — Jesus sometimes says “it is written” (i.e. Scripture) *versus* “you have heard that it was said” (i.e. human tradition). His contrast often aims to show that human traditions must be tested by Scripture, not the other way around.

3. **“Which books exactly?”**  
   — The Jewish Scriptures of His day: Torah (Law), the Prophets, the Writings (Psalms being an example). When He says “Law and Prophets,” that’s how the Jews referred to their canon. citeturn1search11turn1search28turn1search3  
   — He includes Psalms explicitly (in Luke 24:44). citeturn1search8  

4. **“If Scripture can’t be broken, what about apparent contradictions, errors, etc?”**  
   — From Jesus’ perspective, “Scripture cannot be broken” means it can’t be nullified — its authority and core meaning endure. It doesn’t eliminate the need for correct interpretation in context.  

---

### **1. Their Gotcha Question: “What did Jesus identify as Scripture?”**

#### **Materialist Christian Answer:**  
Yes — Jesus identified the Hebrew Scriptures as the Scripture *of His time.* That’s precisely why He fulfilled them instead of adding to them. Historically, the New Testament didn’t exist yet because it hadn’t been *produced by the motion of His ministry.* The Word made flesh speaks, acts, and dies — and the consequence of that event is new Scripture. The Hebrew Israelites freeze the dialectic at one moment of motion, refusing to see that new social reality *creates* new revelation. Christ’s ministry, the Church, and history itself are the continuation of that Scripture’s material unfolding.  

The Bible doesn’t exist as a “list of approved scrolls” but as the written crystallization of a moral transformation in history. Every Christian text from the Gospels onward *is* part of that transformation. When they say, “Jesus never identified Paul’s letters as Scripture,” you respond: of course He didn’t — He hadn’t died yet. History hadn’t moved far enough to write them.

### **2. “Christ only came for Israel.”**  
They’ll quote Matthew 15:24 (“I was sent only to the lost sheep of Israel”) or Matthew 10:5-6 (“Go not into the way of the Gentiles”).  

#### **Materialist Christian Answer:**  
Those verses describe His *initial ministry,* not its final scope. Christ began in Israel because history itself began there — that was the site where the moral consciousness of humanity had first cohered around the idea of a single God, a law, a covenant. But that consciousness could not remain a tribal possession. The dialectic demanded universality. That’s why the *same Jesus* ends the story with “Go therefore and make disciples of all nations.” The motion goes from particular → universal.  

If you freeze the story in its particular moment, you create idolatry of form — nationalism masquerading as religion. The Hebrew Israelites worship the *shell* that carried revelation, mistaking it for the revelation itself. Materialist Christianity sees that “Israel” was a stage in humanity’s self-recognition, not a genetic club.

#### **Materialist Christian Answer:**  
The covenant always begins particular. Every universal truth has a birthplace. But Christ’s fulfillment of that covenant was precisely its transformation — the Law written not on stone or tribe, but on the heart and community. Historically, this fulfilled Jeremiah’s vision by extending the moral law into the social and material structure of every people that lives by justice and love. The New Covenant *materially universalized* what had been tribal.  

If they insist that “Israel” still means literal ethnic Israel, ask them: then what is the point of prophecy being *fulfilled*? If the New Covenant doesn’t transform the old, then Jeremiah was just promising more of the same, not its completion. That would make God a poor storyteller.

#### **Materialist Christian Answer:**  
This is a reactionary fantasy built on misunderstanding the historical function of prophecy. Isaiah’s prophecies were written during exile, a period of national trauma. His language of reversal — nations bowing, captives freed — was meant to restore hope to an oppressed people. So when the Hebrew Israelites say, “You will serve us,” they are repeating Babylon’s language, not God’s. They are *reenacting* the empire’s moral structure under a new flag. The entire point of the Gospel was to invert that hierarchy — to make love the law of rule. The master becomes servant, the king washes feet.

Materially, this means social morality develops from external coercion → internal conviction. Materialist Christianity treats that as dialectical motion: revelation begins exclusive so it can *become* inclusive. To lock the verse in its moment is to deny God’s own motion in history — to turn revelation into stasis.

The Hebrew Israelites confuse permanence of truth with permanence of syntax.  

From a materialist standpoint, “Scripture” is the written residue of revelation’s motion through human society — the fossil record of God’s moral evolution in history.
