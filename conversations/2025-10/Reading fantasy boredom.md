Exactly.  
What you’re describing is **emergent consequence, not moral scoring**—a simulation where behavior has weight because of the web of relationships, not because the designer approves or disapproves.

To reach that, the game engine (even a text engine) just needs three pillars:

---

### 1.  **World memory, not judgment**
Every person, institution, and object remembers facts about the world.  
When you act, you change those facts.

Example  
- You steal a relic → *the abbey* now knows it’s missing.  
- The abbot doesn’t “punish sin”; he begins an inquiry because the object’s value is social and economic.  
- If you fenced it to the blacksmith, and he tells someone, the rumor moves through the memory network until it reaches someone who connects it to you.  
- If no one does, you got away with it—because reality allowed it.

No morality meter, only **state propagation**.

---

### 2.  **Layered perception**
Truth lives in what each actor *believes*, not what actually happened.  
- NPCs act on belief.  
- Your reputation isn’t a number; it’s a set of known claims.  
- Lies work until contradicted by observable reality.  
If someone dies with a secret, that secret dies too.

This means deceit, theft, and faith all obey the same physical law: *information decay.*

---

### 3.  **Material friction**
Every action touches a resource—time, labor, health, trust.  
You can work miracles by burning those resources.  
Nothing stops you from sin; the world simply measures the cost.

So instead of “divine punishment,” it’s:  
> *If you spend faith on deceit, you have less to spend on redemption.*

---

### 4.  **In text**
You and I can model this conversationally:

1. You tell me what you do.  
2. I update the factual world state and what each major actor currently believes.  
3. When events collide (someone cross-checks a belief), consequences emerge naturally.  

You can’t predict every outcome—but you can reason about them, because the logic never changes.
