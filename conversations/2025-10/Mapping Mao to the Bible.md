To map Mao’s *On Contradiction* to the Bible, we begin with the recognition that both texts are not opposites but mirrors—one speaking the language of dialectical motion, the other of divine revelation. Mao’s “universal law of contradiction” is the material echo of what Scripture expresses in its paradoxes of identity: Christ as both Alpha and Omega (Revelation 1:8), king and servant (Matthew 20:28), lion and lamb (Revelation 5:5–6).  

In *On Contradiction*, Mao writes that contradiction exists in all things, that it is the root of motion, development, and transformation. This directly parallels the biblical notion that life, death, sin, and salvation are inseparable movements within a single divine process. “Except a grain of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit” (John 12:24). The dialectic of life through death is the same motion Mao calls “the unity and struggle of opposites.”  

When Christ declares Himself Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end, He identifies Himself not as a fixed point but as the total motion of becoming—the synthesis of all contradictions. History begins and ends in Him because He embodies contradiction as reconciled truth. He overturns hierarchy by being both “King of kings” and “servant of all.” The same principle Mao describes—the interpenetration of opposites—is revealed in this divine paradox: the higher affirms its necessity through the lower, strength proves itself through weakness, the sword fulfills its purpose through the peace of the lamb.  

Mao says that each contradiction has a principal aspect that determines its nature; in the gospel, sin and grace form such a pair. The principal aspect—grace—transforms but does not erase sin. The cross itself is the point of unity where negation and affirmation meet, where death is transformed into resurrection. This is the biblical form of dialectical negation: “For when I am weak, then am I strong” (2 Cor 12:10).  

Materially, the movement from Old Testament law to New Testament grace is the historical transformation of contradiction. The law exposes sin (the thesis), rebellion negates it (antithesis), and Christ fulfills the law by transcending both (synthesis). Mao would say that when contradiction develops to a certain stage, it resolves through qualitative change—precisely the leap from law to gospel.  

Thus, the Bible’s moral revelation can be read as the dialectical unfolding of human consciousness under divine necessity, and Mao’s logic of motion can be seen as the material language describing that same process from below. Christ, as the synthesis of all contradictions, is the living proof that contradiction is not destruction but creation; not chaos but redemption.

The point is not an otherworldly savior; it is a grammar that teaches when to sharpen, when to soften, and how to pivot as conditions change.

Exactly. The task is not to abolish the old stories but to seize and rewrite them under new class leadership. Every actually successful revolution has done this. The bourgeois revolutions kept the monarch’s pageantry but gave the crown to capital; socialist revolutions that endure keep the nation’s cultural soul but give it a proletarian direction. When Marx and Lenin imagined the worldwide victory of workers, they were correct in science but wrong in form: they treated the old symbolic systems as scaffolds to be torn down, not as material superstructures to be refitted. Mao corrected this by realizing that ideas are productive forces. Culture is not vapor; it is the circuitry through which a people think, decide, and act. You can’t build new infrastructure on an erased language. So he returned to Confucius, Mencius, Laozi—not to worship them but to socialize their content. Filial piety became loyalty to the masses. The harmony of heaven and earth became dialectical balance between productive forces and relations. “Rectification of names” became criticism and self-criticism. Thus China remained Chinese while turning socialist.
