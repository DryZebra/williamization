The commenter is making two moves at once:

### 1. **“Capitalism as currently defined by the derivative market”**
- They’re implying that when you say *“land always retains value, it’s just a waiting game”*, you’re not describing capitalism in its broad sense (commodity production, wage labor, capital accumulation).  
- Instead, they’re saying you’re describing **financialized capitalism**—capitalism in its current stage, where value is determined not by direct production but by speculative instruments like derivatives, mortgage-backed securities, etc.  
- In other words, they’re framing your position as: *“You think property will always hold value because the market has been structured (via derivatives, speculation, financial engineering) to make it appear so.”*  

So, *“capitalism as currently defined”* = capitalism in its **late financialized form**, not capitalism in general. They’re trying to narrow your claim down.

---

### 2. **“Germany said the same thing in 1913–1916”**
- This is a historical jab.  
- Pre–WWI Germany was heavily industrialized, with speculative bubbles in land and financial markets. The belief then was similar: *“land always appreciates, value always returns, downturns are temporary.”*  
- Then **WWI, inflation, and later hyperinflation (1920s)** destroyed that assumption. Property values *collapsed* relative to currency value, and speculation on land left people ruined.  
- So the commenter is saying: *“You’re just repeating the same complacent logic that crumbled in Germany during 1913–16.”*  

---

### 3. **How to read it back to them**
They’re basically telling you: *“You’re defending the current speculative form of capitalism, not some eternal truth. History already disproved your kind of optimism when Germany thought the same way and then got wrecked.”*

- **During the German hyperinflation**, the *currency* collapsed, so prices in marks skyrocketed. But that didn’t mean land itself “lost value” — it meant the unit of measurement collapsed. If anything, land became one of the safest places to park wealth, because it retained *use-value* when paper money became worthless.  
- **In real terms (relative to goods, labor, and productive capacity)**, the cost of land in Germany before WWI compared to the cost after stabilization in the late 1920s was higher, not lower. Owners who held onto property through the inflation preserved value better than those who held cash or bonds.  
- **Across the 1900s**, even with two world wars, occupation, partition, and reunification, German land values steadily increased over the long arc. Temporary collapses in paper prices or speculative bubbles didn’t erase the fact that real estate always recovered and then exceeded previous levels once stability returned.
