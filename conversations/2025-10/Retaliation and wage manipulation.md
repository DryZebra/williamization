Excellent question — and you’re thinking like a real steward here.  
Let’s break it down clearly in plain terms.  

---

### ⚖️ 1. Their “compliance” excuse *doesn’t erase liability.*
If management says, “we had to change it because it wasn’t compliant,” they’re **admitting the old system violated wage law** — likely the *Fair Labor Standards Act* (FLSA) rule that non-discretionary bonuses must be included when calculating overtime.  

That means for every hour of overtime worked while the $10/hour “bonus” existed, they likely **underpaid** the overtime rate, because they didn’t recalculate time-and-a-half using the true “regular rate” (base + bonus).  

👉 That creates a **back-pay liability** for the entire period that structure existed, regardless of what they pay going forward.

---

### 🧾 2. “Making it equal now” doesn’t erase past violations.
Changing the formula so it “balances out” today only affects *future* pay periods.  
The FLSA and state law don’t allow an employer to retroactively “offset” or “fix” illegal underpayment by adjusting future pay. Each pay period stands on its own.  

So even if the *weekly totals* roughly match now, the *previous underpaid OT hours* are still owed, plus **liquidated damages** (essentially double pay) if found willful.

---

### 🪓 3. How to pursue it.
- Under the **CBA**, that’s a grievance for *improper wage calculation and retroactive pay*.  
- Under the **law**, it’s a potential **wage-and-hour claim**. The union can push it through the grievance process first; if unresolved, the DOL Wage and Hour Division (or a private attorney) can pursue back pay.  

You’d phrase it like:
> “Employer’s prior pay structure failed to include non-discretionary $10/hr bonus in regular rate for overtime calculations, resulting in underpayment for all overtime hours worked during that period. We seek full restitution of unpaid overtime premiums plus applicable damages.”
