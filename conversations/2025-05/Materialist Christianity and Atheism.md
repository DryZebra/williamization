### 1. **Evidential Problem of Evil**  
**Surface Argument**:  
The amount and nature of suffering—especially of the innocent—makes the existence of an all-powerful, all-loving God *unlikely*. Not impossible (like in the Logical Problem of Evil), but improbable.

**Materialist Christian Root Approach**:

We ask: *Who needs this argument?* And *why?*

This argument doesn’t emerge in premodern peasant societies or tribal cultures. It emerges in a literate, bureaucratic, capitalist society where:
- Evil is bureaucratized.
- Suffering is senselessly distributed.
- People are **isolated**, not just physically, but epistemically.
- Institutions claiming divine authority (e.g., Churches) are complicit in that suffering.

So this isn’t a metaphysical argument—it’s a class consciousness response to the contradiction between *promised meaning* and *observed cruelty*.

**Reframed through Materialist Christianity**:
The suffering is real. But what’s being rejected here is *not God*, it’s the **lie** about God told by imperial institutions. The God that was supposed to act as a moral policeman for the powerful has failed. And good. Because that god never existed.

Materialist Christianity doesn’t defend the metaphysical “all-loving God.” It asks instead:  
> *What moral force emerged dialectically in opposition to empire, suffering, and systemic evil?*

The Bible does not deny suffering—it elevates it. Not as redemptive metaphysics, but as a **material witness to contradiction**. In the Book of Job, God does not resolve suffering—he exposes that logic itself cannot.

So instead of seeing the **Evidential Problem of Evil** as an argument *against* Christianity, we see it as the *engine that birthed Christianity*. The contradiction created the need for new moral synthesis. That’s what Jesus represented.
