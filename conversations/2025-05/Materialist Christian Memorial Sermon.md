**Materialist Christian Sermon for Memorial Day**  
*Title: “What We Carry”*

Brothers, sisters, comrades—today is Memorial Day. A day draped in flags, in folded uniforms, in names etched into stone. It’s a day the system would have us remember the dead in the way it prefers: with silence, obedience, and unquestioned repetition. But we are Materialist Christians. We do not kneel to appearances. We test memory against motion. We do not ask what is sacred. We ask what *moves.*

And so we ask: do the rituals of this day *still* preserve the motion they were meant to carry? Or have they become hollow—obedience dressed in nostalgia?

Because every structure we inherit—every flag, every verse, every grave—must be tested. Not for loyalty. But for *consequence.* Do they still move trust through the body of the people? Or do they extract it and sell it back?

The cross is not sacred because it’s old. It is sacred because it keeps coming back *when we suffer without being seen.* It is a pressure field, not a belief. And so is the battlefield. And so is the mother who labors alone while a folded flag sits on her shelf. These are not symbols. They are *sub-objects.* Structures that continue to shape behavior, whether or not we still remember why.

What we call Memorial Day is not simply about the fallen. It is about what they *moved for.* And the tragedy is not just that they died. The tragedy is that *their motion was hijacked.* The Gmorknic coup captured the resonance of their sacrifice and reversed it. The image of the soldier was hollowed out and used to sell oil, to guard banks, to bomb villages where no enemy had ever drawn breath against us. This is not remembrance. This is *inversion.*

But that does not mean memory is dead. Because not all resonance can be stolen. Some motion resists capture. The young Black man who enlisted to feed his family. The immigrant who believed the country could change. The working-class kid who had no options but chose to protect the one next to him. These are not slogans. They are *moral labor.* And labor *echoes.*

So today we remember not the story told on television, but the *real story.* The field of pressure that still radiates from sacrifice that was not returned. The graves of the poor who died in rich men’s wars. The bodies used up and left behind. But also: the resistance that reemerges. The field medic who treated all, friend and foe. The squad that protected civilians against orders. The soldier who wrote home not about glory, but about doubt, about loss, about the cost of obedience to a hollowed flag.

This day does not belong to empire. It belongs to *rupture.*

Memorial Day should not be a monument. It should be a *mirror.* Not to glorify the dead, but to ask: what do *we* now carry? Do we carry the contradiction forward? Or do we finally break the cycle?

To honor the dead is not to obey what killed them. It is to finish the work they could not.

And so we say this, in the name of every broken body used as fuel for empire:

Let the dead bury the dead. But let the living walk forward with only what still moves.

Let us carry the real weight of their motion—not with slogans, not with pageantry, but with labor. With consequence. With the resurrection of *trust* not in flag or uniform, but in one another.

Because the only true memorial… is to build the world they *should have died for.*

Amen.
