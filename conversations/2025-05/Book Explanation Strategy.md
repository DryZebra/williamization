Here’s a chapter-by-chapter walk-through you can give your pastor if the conversation gets deeper and you want to really show the structure of your book:

---

**“Well, it starts by asking, what is real?”**  
I argue that what’s *real* isn’t what we can touch, but what causes change—especially in how people act. Things like money, law, marriage, and even God—those are real not because they have atoms, but because they have consequences. I call this behavioral gravity. If it moves people, it's real.

**“Then it moves into motion.”**  
The second chapter says that motion is more real than things. Everything we think of as stable is just paused motion. Even faith, rituals, institutions—they don’t survive because they’re true in a static sense. They survive because they keep moving. That’s what keeps them alive.

**“Then we get to the concept of the sub-object.”**  
That’s a third category of reality—not an object like a rock, not a subject like a person, but a structure that exists between people. Like ‘marriage’ or ‘respect’ or ‘the Bible.’ These are real because of behavior, not belief. They organize human action. They persist across time through repetition and consequence.

**“Chapter 4 ties morality to labor.”**  
I define morality as the value of human behavior over time—what keeps people alive together across rupture. If a tradition survives contradiction while preserving dignity, then it’s moral. If it calcifies or becomes extractive, it isn’t. Morality becomes about what we preserve and maintain with our actions.

**“Chapter 5 is about trust.”**  
Trust becomes the universal currency of moral systems. Not money. Not doctrine. Trust. And trust isn’t belief—it’s repeated, risk-bearing behavior over time. Betrayal hurts more than failure because it breaks motion. And that motion is what holds structures together.

**“Then I introduce the idea of sub-object resonance.”**  
Chapter 6 talks about why symbols like the cross, or even the hammer and sickle, still move people. It’s not belief. It’s the field of consequence around them. That’s why some objects feel ‘charged.’ Their history echoes in our behavior. I call this resonance. And some resonance can be hijacked—that’s what I call ‘Gmorknicity’—but some of it resists manipulation and keeps reasserting itself, like the Gospel under persecution【9†Sub-Object Resonance (Gmorknicity)】.

**“Chapter 7 is where I talk about the Bible.”**  
I treat the Bible as a container of moral capital—stored labor, trust, and contradiction across thousands of years. It doesn’t endure because it’s divine, but because it keeps adapting, surviving, organizing life across time. People argue with it, reenter it, reinterpret it. And that’s why it’s sacred.

**“Chapter 8 is about Christianity itself.”**  
I look at it as the most successful moral infrastructure in history. Not because everyone believed it, but because it encoded resurrection into its very structure. Forgiveness, death, rebirth, hierarchy and love—it absorbs contradiction without dissolving. That’s rare.

**“Chapter 9 compares Christianity to capitalism.”**  
I show how both systems abstract trust from labor. Capitalism creates fake trust—branding, credit, performance. Christianity created real trust, at least at first, through moral labor. But now both are showing signs of decay. Ritual without consequence. Symbols without care.

**“Chapter 10 explains how these structures live and die.”**  
Sub-objects go through life cycles: emergence, alignment, decay, rupture, and rebirth. The resurrection isn’t just a story—it’s a pattern. Any structure that can survive contradiction without losing its moral motion gets reborn. That’s why I say resurrection isn’t metaphor. It’s motion.

**“The final chapter lays out Moral Communism.”**  
I’m arguing for a system that not only reorganizes economics, but preserves moral motion. A system that stabilizes trust, care, and survival without demanding obedience or loyalty to dead forms. Something that grows, adapts, reenters history—not through force, but through moral labor.
