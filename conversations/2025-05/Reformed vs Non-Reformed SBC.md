## Assistant

The difference between **Reformed** and **non-Reformed Southern Baptists** lies in their theological roots—especially regarding **salvation**, **God's sovereignty**, and **free will**.

### Reformed Southern Baptists:
- **Calvinist influence**: They embrace key tenets of Reformed theology, often summarized in the acronym **TULIP**:
  - **Total Depravity** – humans are completely incapable of choosing God without divine intervention.
  - **Unconditional Election** – God chooses who will be saved based on His will alone, not foreseen merit or faith.
  - **Limited Atonement** – Christ died specifically for the elect.
  - **Irresistible Grace** – those God calls will inevitably come to faith.
  - **Perseverance of the Saints** – true believers will endure in faith to the end.
- They often cite the **1689 London Baptist Confession** or the **Abstract of Principles** used by Southern Seminary.
- Worship and preaching tend to emphasize **God's sovereignty**, **biblical exposition**, and **doctrinal precision**.

### Non-Reformed Southern Baptists:
- More closely aligned with **Traditionalist** or **Arminian**-leaning theology:
  - Believe in **genuine human free will** to accept or reject the Gospel.
  - See **God's foreknowledge** as distinct from predestination—He knows who will believe but does not predetermine it.
  - Emphasize **whosoever will** evangelism—salvation is offered to all, and any may respond.
  - Often more pragmatic or revivalist in their preaching style (e.g., altar calls, personal decisions).
- Generally reject Limited Atonement and Irresistible Grace as unbiblical.

### Within the SBC:
- Both views exist under the broader **Southern Baptist Convention (SBC)** umbrella.
- Tensions sometimes flare in seminaries and denominational leadership, but the SBC does not require adherence to either side.
- The Baptist Faith and Message 2000 (BF&M 2000), the SBC’s official statement, leaves space for both positions, though it leans more toward the non-Reformed, free-will language.

In short: **Reformed Southern Baptists believe God unilaterally chooses who will be saved**, while **non-Reformed Southern Baptists believe each person can freely respond to the Gospel.**

## User

So for reformed they believe that people are predestined for hell?

## Assistant

Yes—**Reformed theology** (especially in its Calvinist form) teaches **double predestination**, which means:

1. **God predestines some people to be saved** (the elect), and  
2. **God passes over or ordains others to damnation** (the reprobate).

This isn't always framed as "God actively sends people to hell" in the same way He chooses some for heaven. Instead, Reformed thinkers often say God **withholds saving grace** from the non-elect. The logic is:

- **All people deserve damnation** due to original sin and total depravity.
- God, in His mercy, **chooses to save some**, not based on anything they've done.
- Those not chosen are **left in their sin**, which they still freely choose—but God does not intervene to save them.

So yes, under Reformed theology:
- God *elects* some to eternal life (unconditionally),
- And God *passes over* others, resulting in eternal punishment.

This is why Reformed preachers emphasize **God’s justice and mercy**, not fairness in a human sense. Everyone deserves hell; the shock is that anyone is saved at all.

Non-Reformed Southern Baptists tend to **reject this view**, seeing it as inconsistent with God's love and the free offer of the Gospel.

## Assistant

In a typical **Southern Baptist** church like **Oak Grove Baptist Church (OGBC)** in Mebane, NC, the process of selecting a pastor follows a **congregational and autonomous model**, meaning the local church body—not an external hierarchy—makes the final decision. While each church is independent and may have its own unique process, the general structure usually goes like this:

### 1. **Pastor Search Committee is Formed**
- The deacons or elders will often nominate a **Pastor Search Committee**, which is then approved by a **congregational vote**.
- This committee is made up of respected members who reflect the diversity of the church body (age, gender, ministry roles, etc.).

### 2. **Defining the Role and Qualifications**
- The committee, with church input, outlines qualifications, theological positions, experience, and the desired direction for the church (e.g., evangelism, youth focus, Reformed leaning, etc.).
- They often specify adherence to the **Baptist Faith and Message 2000** and may clarify whether they want a Reformed-leaning pastor or not.

### 3. **Collecting and Reviewing Applications**
- Resumes are collected through Baptist networks (like state conventions or seminaries) and public listings.
- The committee reviews **doctrinal statements, sermons, education**, and references.

### 4. **Interviews and Visits**
- Promising candidates are interviewed and often invited to **preach a guest sermon** (sometimes without knowing they're a candidate).
- The committee may attend the candidate’s current church or watch videos to evaluate preaching style and pastoral leadership.

### 5. **Narrowing Down and Extended Invitation**
- Once a finalist is selected, the candidate is **invited to preach a trial sermon**.
- Afterward, a **formal vote by the congregation** is held (usually requiring a supermajority—70% to 90% approval depending on church bylaws).

### 6. **Call and Installation**
- If approved, the church formally **calls the pastor**, and the candidate accepts or declines.
- Once accepted, an **installation service** may be held to recognize the new leadership.

### Important Note:
Because OGBC is part of the **Southern Baptist Convention**, they have **no obligation to follow any external authority**—not even the SBC. So ultimately, it’s up to **their members and governing documents** (bylaws or constitution). If you want to know exactly how OGBC does it, you could ask for their **church constitution or bylaws**, which typically spell out the procedure.
